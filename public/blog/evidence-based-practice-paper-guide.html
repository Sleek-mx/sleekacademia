How to Write an Evidence-Based Practice Paper

From PICO question to evidence synthesis: a practical walkthrough for nursing students

July 2026 • 10 min read

By the Sleek Academia tutoring team

menu_bookPart of our Assignment Help for Nursing Students →

The evidence-based practice paper is the assignment nursing instructors use to test whether you can do the thing modern nursing actually runs on: find the best available evidence, judge its quality, and translate it into a recommendation for patient care. It is also the assignment students most often get wrong, usually by writing a summary of five articles instead of an argument built from evidence.

The difference matters. A summary tells the reader what each study said. An EBP paper tells the reader what the evidence, taken together, means for practice. This guide walks through the full process: building a PICO question, searching the literature, ranking the evidence, appraising what you find, and writing the implementation section that most students underdevelop.

Key Takeaways

  • Build a precise PICO question before you search; it controls everything downstream.
  • Search CINAHL and PubMed systematically, and document your search terms and filters.
  • Rank your sources against the evidence hierarchy and favor systematic reviews and RCTs.
  • Synthesize findings across studies instead of summarizing study by study.
  • Close with a concrete implementation plan, not a vague call for more research.

Start With a PICO Question That Actually Works

Every strong EBP paper begins with a focused clinical question, and the PICO framework is the standard tool for building one. PICO stands for Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome. Each element forces you to narrow your scope:

  • Population: Who exactly are you studying? "Adults with type 2 diabetes" is workable. "Patients" is not.
  • Intervention: What practice, treatment, or change are you evaluating? Be specific about the intervention, not the disease.
  • Comparison: What is the alternative? This is usually standard care, a different intervention, or no intervention.
  • Outcome: What measurable result do you care about? Infection rates, pain scores, readmission rates, and quality-of-life measures all work.

A weak question looks like this: "Does exercise help diabetes?" A PICO-built version looks like this: "In adults with type 2 diabetes (P), does a structured aerobic exercise program (I), compared with standard care alone (C), improve glycemic control as measured by HbA1c (O)?" The second version tells you exactly what to search, which studies to include, and what outcome to compare across them. Some courses add a T for time frame, making it PICOT, for example "over twelve weeks."

Test your question before you commit: search a database with its key terms. If you get thousands of results, narrow the population or outcome. If you get almost none, broaden the intervention or choose a related outcome.

Searching the Literature Systematically

Instructors can tell when a search was done in ten minutes. A defensible search is systematic, and your paper should describe it briefly: which databases you searched, which terms you used, and which limits you applied.

Where to search

CINAHL is the core nursing database and should be your first stop. PubMed covers the broader medical literature. The Cochrane Library holds systematic reviews, which sit at the top of the evidence hierarchy. PsycINFO is useful for behavioral and mental health topics. Google Scholar is acceptable as a supplement but not as your primary source, because it lacks consistent peer-review filtering.

How to search

Derive your search terms from the PICO elements. Combine synonyms with OR and concepts with AND: ("type 2 diabetes" OR "T2DM") AND ("aerobic exercise" OR "physical activity") AND ("HbA1c" OR "glycemic control"). Then apply limits that match your assignment: publication within the last five years, English language, peer-reviewed, and adult populations where relevant. Save the search history. When you write the paper, you will summarize this process in two or three sentences, and some rubrics ask for a PRISMA-style flow of how many results you found, screened, and included.

Rank Your Sources on the Evidence Hierarchy

Not all evidence carries equal weight, and an EBP paper must show that you know the difference. The standard hierarchy, from strongest to weakest:

  1. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials
  2. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs)
  3. Cohort studies
  4. Case-control studies
  5. Case series and case reports
  6. Expert opinion and narrative reviews

You will not always find level-one evidence for your question, and that is acceptable. What is not acceptable is hiding where your sources sit. State the level of evidence for your key sources explicitly. A paper that relies on cohort studies and says so, with reasoning, reads as more credible than one that treats a case report like an RCT.

Appraise, Do Not Just Summarize

Critical appraisal is the step most students skip, and it is the step rubrics reward most heavily. For each key study, ask three questions:

  • Is it valid? Consider sample size, randomization, blinding, control of confounders, and dropout rates. A 24-person unblinded trial does not carry the same weight as a 2,000-person multi-site RCT.
  • Are the results meaningful? Look beyond statistical significance to effect size and clinical significance. A statistically significant HbA1c reduction of 0.1 percent may not change practice.
  • Does it apply to my population? A study of hospitalized older adults may not generalize to community-dwelling younger adults with the same condition.

Many programs provide an appraisal tool, such as the CASP checklists or the Johns Hopkins evidence appraisal forms. If your course assigns one, use it and reference it. If not, the three questions above cover the essentials.

Synthesize the Evidence

Synthesis is where the paper becomes an argument. Instead of devoting one paragraph to each study in isolation, organize your findings around themes or outcomes and bring the studies into conversation. For example: "Three of the four studies reported significant HbA1c reductions with structured exercise (Lee et al., 2023; Nguyen et al., 2024; Osei & Baker, 2025), although the effect was smallest in the study with the shortest follow-up (Nguyen et al., 2024)." That single sentence compares, weighs, and locates agreement and tension across the evidence.

A simple evidence table can help you and your reader. Columns typically include author and year, design, sample, intervention, key findings, and level of evidence. Build it while you read, and the synthesis section becomes far easier to write. Keep all citations in APA format; our APA formatting guide for nursing students covers the details.

Write a Real Implementation Section

The weakest EBP papers end with "more research is needed." The strongest answer the question the assignment actually asks: what should practice do with this evidence? Your implementation section should cover four elements:

  1. The recommendation. State it plainly: based on the evidence, should the intervention be adopted, modified, or rejected for this population?
  2. Barriers and facilitators. Cost, staffing, training, patient preferences, and organizational culture all determine whether a recommendation survives contact with a real unit.
  3. Evaluation. How would you know the change worked? Name the outcome metric, the measurement method, and the time frame.
  4. Nursing role. EBP is a nursing competency. Specify what the bedside nurse, the nurse educator, or the unit council does in this plan.

Ground this section in a realistic setting, such as a medical-surgical unit or a community clinic. Specificity is what separates a recommendation from a wish.

A Structure You Can Reuse

Most EBP papers follow this skeleton: introduction and clinical significance, the PICO question, search strategy, critical appraisal of the evidence, synthesis of findings, practice recommendation and implementation plan, and conclusion. Map each section to your rubric's weighting. Appraisal and synthesis usually carry the most marks, so give them the most space and the most care.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a PICO question in nursing?

PICO is a framework for building a focused clinical question: Population (who you are studying), Intervention (what you are testing), Comparison (what you are measuring it against), and Outcome (what changes as a result). A well-built PICO question drives your entire literature search and keeps the paper focused.

How many sources do I need for an EBP paper?

Most undergraduate EBP papers expect five to ten peer-reviewed sources, with at least some at the higher levels of the evidence hierarchy, such as systematic reviews or randomized controlled trials. Always follow your rubric, and prioritize quality and relevance over raw count.

Which databases should I search for nursing evidence?

CINAHL and PubMed are the two core databases for nursing research. The Cochrane Library is the best source for systematic reviews, and PsycINFO is useful for behavioral and mental health topics. Google Scholar can supplement these but should not replace them.

What is the difference between an EBP paper and a research paper?

A research paper reports a single study you designed and conducted. An EBP paper synthesizes existing published evidence to answer a clinical question and recommend a practice change. EBP papers appraise and compare multiple studies rather than generating new data.
