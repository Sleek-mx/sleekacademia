How to Write a Legal Memo Using the IRAC Method

Issue, rule, application, conclusion — the structure every law school and firm expects, with a worked example

July 2026 • 10 min read

By the Sleek Academia tutoring team

menu_bookPreparing for the bar too? See The UBE Decoded →

The legal memo is the first document law students are graded on and the one junior associates actually write all day. It answers a supervising attorney's question — can our client win this, and why? — with an honest, source-backed prediction. Not persuasion. Prediction. That single distinction drives every structural choice in the document, and forgetting it is why first-semester memos come back covered in comments. This guide breaks down the standard memo structure, the IRAC method that powers its analysis, and a short worked example you can model.

Objective, Not Persuasive: The Memo Mindset

A memo is written for your own side. Its value lies in telling the truth about a case's weaknesses before the other side does. A brief or motion, by contrast, is advocacy: it argues one position as forcefully as the law allows. Students who write memos like briefs — hiding bad facts, ignoring contrary authority — produce documents that are useless to the reader and easy for a professor to dismantle. In a memo, you present the strongest version of the opposing argument and then explain, candidly, how it affects the likely outcome.

The Standard Memo Structure

Formats vary slightly by school and firm, but the skeleton is remarkably stable:

SectionWhat it does
Heading blockTo, From, Date, and a Re: line that names the client and the issue precisely
Question PresentedThe legal question, with the key facts embedded, in one sentence
Brief AnswerThe predicted outcome in a few sentences, with the core reasoning
Statement of FactsThe legally relevant facts, told neutrally, in narrative order
DiscussionThe analysis: one full IRAC cycle per issue, with counterarguments
ConclusionThe prediction restated, plus recommended next steps

IRAC: The Engine of the Discussion

IRAC — Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion — is not a school gimmick. It is how legal analysis actually proceeds, and it maps directly onto what the bar exam's essay section rewards. If you are preparing for the UBE, the same discipline applies there; our UBE breakdown covers how the exam scores it.

  • Issue. One sentence framing the precise legal question for this sub-part: "The issue is whether the store breached its duty of care to the customer."
  • Rule. The governing law, stated accurately and cited: the elements of negligence, the controlling statute, the precedent that defines the standard. Include rule explanation — a sentence or two showing how courts have applied the rule — drawn from your cited cases.
  • Application. The heart of the memo. Apply each element of the rule to your facts, arguing both sides where the outcome is genuinely contestable. Compare your facts to the precedent facts: same pattern, distinguishable, or somewhere between.
  • Conclusion. The likely outcome for that issue, stated with an appropriate degree of confidence: "A court will likely find breach, because..."

Multi-issue problems get multiple IRACs, one after another, each under its own heading. A negligence analysis might run four cycles: duty, breach, causation, and damages.

Writing the Question Presented and Brief Answer

The Question Presented compresses law and facts into one sentence. A reliable formula is under / does / when: "Under Virginia negligence law, does a grocery store breach its duty of care to an invitee when an employee mops an aisle, leaves the floor wet for twenty minutes, and posts no warning sign, causing a customer to fall?" Everything the answer turns on is in the question — that is the test of a good one.

The Brief Answer mirrors it: "Probably yes. A store owes invitees a duty of reasonable care in maintaining the premises. Leaving a freshly mopped floor unmarked for twenty minutes likely falls below that standard, and the fall caused quantifiable injury. The store may argue the hazard was open and obvious, but the twenty-minute lapse and absence of any warning make breach the more probable finding." Short, confident, and hedged exactly where the law is uncertain.

The Statement of Facts: Relevant and Neutral

Include only facts that are legally relevant (they affect an element of the rule) or background necessary to follow the story. Tell it chronologically, in the third person, with no adjectives doing argumentative work. "The customer walked briskly because she was late" suggests comparative fault and does not belong in a neutral recitation. "The customer entered the aisle at approximately 2:10 p.m." does. If you find yourself characterizing facts, you have slipped into advocacy — move that energy to the Discussion, where it belongs.

Finding the Rule: Mandatory vs. Persuasive Authority

Before you can state the rule, you have to know which rules bind. Mandatory (binding) authority is the law a court must follow: the constitution, statutes, and regulations of the jurisdiction, plus the decisions of the appellate courts above it. Persuasive authority is everything else — cases from other states, lower courts, treatises, law review articles — which a court may find convincing but need not obey. Build your rule statement from mandatory authority first, always, and reach for persuasive sources only to fill genuine gaps or to show how other jurisdictions have handled a novel question. In research terms: start with the statute, read the controlling cases that interpret its key terms, and let secondary sources (a good treatise or ALR annotation) orient you at the very beginning rather than serve as your citations. Citing a law review when a controlling case exists is a first-semester tell.

The Discussion, in Detail

Here is the discipline that separates strong memos from plot summaries:

  1. Lead with your thesis sentence. Each issue opens with the conclusion, not the history of the doctrine. "The store likely breached its duty of care."
  2. State the rule completely before applying it. Elements, standards, and the precedent that fleshes them out, cited properly. Use rule explanation from real cases: "In Marshall v. Foodmart, the court found breach where a spill remained for fifteen minutes without warning."
  3. Apply by comparison. The strongest application technique is factual analogy: our twenty minutes exceeds the fifteen that sufficed in Marshall, and like that defendant, our client posted no sign. Then take the other side seriously: the store will argue the wet floor was open and obvious, distinguishing Marshall where the spill was clear liquid in dim light.
  4. Resolve it. Do not end at "on the other hand." Weigh the competing readings and commit to the more probable outcome, with the reason.

A Short Worked Example

Putting the pieces together, one issue in miniature:

Issue: Whether the store breached its duty of care to the customer. Rule: Property owners owe invitees a duty of reasonable care to maintain safe premises and warn of hidden hazards. Marshall v. Foodmart held that a spill left fifteen minutes without a warning sign breached that duty. Application: Here, the aisle floor remained wet for twenty minutes after mopping, five minutes longer than the spill in Marshall, and no sign, cone, or verbal warning was provided. The store will contend the hazard was open and obvious because the floor glistened under bright lighting; but the customer had no reason to anticipate a freshly mopped floor in a dry-goods aisle, and the absence of any warning eliminates the store's strongest defense. Conclusion: A court will likely find the store breached its duty of care.

Citation Basics for Memos

Legal citations run on The Bluebook (or ALWD, where your school specifies). The core case citation format is: Case Name, Volume Reporter Page (Court Year) — Marshall v. Foodmart, 214 Va. 350 (1998). Italicize or underline case names, use "id." for immediately preceding citations, and learn the signals early: see for support, cf. for comparison, but see for contradiction. Whatever system you use, consistency is what gets graded first. For non-legal sources in interdisciplinary papers, our APA 7 guide covers the other format you are likely to meet.

Two more conventions matter early. First, pin cites: when you quote or rely on a specific holding, cite the exact page — Marshall, 214 Va. at 355 — because a citation without a pin tells the reader nothing verifiable. Second, quotations from cases follow the same discipline as quotations anywhere: quote the precise language that matters (a holding's operative phrase), never a paragraph of the court's background recitation. Block quotes of fifty words or more are permitted but should be rare; if a case's reasoning cannot be compressed into your own sentence with a pin cite, you probably have not finished understanding it yet.

CREAC: IRAC's Office-Ready Cousin

Once you move from the classroom into practice, you will meet CREAC — Conclusion, Rule, Explanation, Application, Conclusion. It is the same logic as IRAC with the answer moved up front and case-based rule explanation broken out as its own step: state your conclusion on the issue, state the governing rule, explain the rule through the precedent (how courts have applied it), apply it to your facts, and conclude again. Most firms and many professors now prefer it because the reader never waits for the point. If your assignment specifies IRAC, use IRAC; if it is silent, CREAC's answer-first structure is the safer habit to build. Either way, the discipline is identical: complete rules, honest application, committed conclusions.

A Memo Skeleton You Can Copy

When the document feels abstract, start from this structure and fill it in:

  • To / From / Date / Re: [Client] — [issue in three to six words]
  • Question Presented: Under [jurisdiction's law], does [legal standard] when [the two or three facts the answer turns on]?
  • Brief Answer: [Probably yes/no]. [Core rule in one sentence]. [Why the facts satisfy or fail it, in two sentences]. [The strongest contrary point and why it does not change the outcome].
  • Facts: [Chronological, neutral, relevant-only narrative].
  • Discussion: [Issue 1 heading — full IRAC or CREAC] ... [Issue 2 heading — full IRAC or CREAC].
  • Conclusion: [Prediction restated in light of the analysis, plus recommended next steps for the client].

Tone and Style: How Legal Writing Actually Sounds

Students often reach for legalese because it feels authoritative, and it reliably produces the opposite effect. Modern legal writing prizes plain English: short declarative sentences, active voice, and ordinary words. Write "because," not "inasmuch as"; "before," not "prior to"; "the store breached its duty," not "it would appear that a breach of duty may have potentially occurred." Hedging has its place — genuine uncertainty should be stated precisely ("the courts are split, and this jurisdiction has not addressed the question") — but vague hedging everywhere reads as a writer who has not decided anything. Cut the throat-clearing openings ("It is important to note that..."), keep one idea per sentence, and let the analysis, not the adverbs, carry the confidence.

One related habit: read the assignment's framing before you write a word. Who is the audience — a supervising attorney, a judge's clerk, a client? What jurisdiction controls? Is the question narrow ("does the exclusionary rule apply to this search") or open ("assess our client's exposure")? A memo that answers a slightly different question than the one asked is wrong no matter how well it is written.

The Mistakes That Cost the Most Points

  • Case summaries instead of analysis. Retelling what happened in Marshall for a full paragraph without connecting it to your facts is the classic 1L error.
  • Hiding the counterargument. If the other side's best point appears nowhere in your memo, the document has failed its purpose.
  • Conclusory application. "The store breached its duty because it was unreasonable" restates the question. Show the element-by-element reasoning.
  • Advocacy in the facts. Loaded verbs and adjectives in the Statement of Facts signal an unreliable narrator.
  • Burying the answer. The Brief Answer belongs on page one. Busy readers decide whether to keep reading based on it.

Memos reward discipline more than brilliance: neutral facts, a precise question, complete rules, honest application, committed conclusions. Build the habit now and it will serve you through law school, the bar, and practice. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a legal memo and a legal brief?

A legal memo is an objective internal document that analyzes both sides of an issue and predicts an outcome. A brief is a persuasive document filed with a court that argues one side. Memos inform; briefs advocate.

Do I use IRAC for every issue in the memo?

Yes. Each distinct legal issue gets its own IRAC cycle inside the discussion section: state the issue, lay out the governing rule, apply it to your facts, and conclude. Multi-issue memos are essentially several IRACs in sequence.

How long should a legal memo be?

Law school memos typically run five to ten pages, and office memos aim for the shortest length that fully answers the question. The discussion section should take up most of the document.

What citation format does a legal memo use?

Most US law schools and firms use The Bluebook, though some jurisdictions and schools use ALWD or local rules. Follow your assignment or office convention, and be consistent.

Should a legal memo include counterarguments?

Yes. A memo's job is an honest prediction, and that requires presenting the strongest arguments against your conclusion and explaining why they do or do not change the outcome. Omitting counterarguments is the most common memo weakness.
