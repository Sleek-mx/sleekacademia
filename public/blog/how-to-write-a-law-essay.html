How to Write a Law Essay: Structure and Tips

From decoding the question to building IRAC paragraphs and handling counterarguments

July 2026 • 10 min read

By the Sleek Academia tutoring team

menu_bookPart of our Assignment Help for Law Students →

Legal writing feels foreign at first because it is not essay writing in the way other disciplines mean it. A law essay is not judged on how elegantly you express an opinion. It is judged on whether you can identify the legal issues hidden in a fact pattern or question, state the governing law accurately, and apply that law to the facts with disciplined reasoning. Once you understand that graders are scoring analysis rather than style, the path to a strong essay becomes mechanical in the best sense of the word.

This guide covers the full process: recognizing what the question is really asking, researching efficiently, building a thesis, writing IRAC paragraphs, handling counterarguments, and finishing with a conclusion that earns its marks.

Key Takeaways

  • Identify the question type first: issue-spotter, policy question, or critical evaluation.
  • State a defensible thesis in the introduction and preview your structure.
  • Build body paragraphs on IRAC: Issue, Rule, Application, Conclusion.
  • The application step is where marks live; spend the most words there.
  • Address the strongest counterargument directly rather than ignoring it.

Step 1: Identify the Type of Question You Are Being Asked

Law essay questions come in three broad types, and each demands a different approach:

  • The issue-spotter (problem question). You get a fact pattern and must identify the legal issues, apply the law, and advise a party. These are the staple of law school exams. Your job is comprehensive coverage: find every viable claim, defense, and threshold issue, then analyze each one.
  • The policy or evaluative question. "Should the law recognize X?" or "Critically evaluate the rule in Y." Here the law itself is the subject. You need a thesis about whether the rule is justified, supported by cases, scholarship, and policy reasoning.
  • The critical essay. Common in jurisprudence and electives, these ask you to evaluate a theory, a judgment, or a line of authority. Close reading of a small number of sources matters more than breadth.

Underline the instruction words: advise, discuss, evaluate, compare, critically analyze. Each verb defines the deliverable. "Advise" means reach a practical conclusion for a client. "Discuss" means present competing views before weighing in. Misreading the verb is the single most common reason otherwise competent essays underperform.

Step 2: Research With a Plan, Not a Net

Legal research rewards sequence. Start with secondary sources to orient yourself: a treatise, a practice guide, or a recent law review article gives you the doctrinal map and points you to the leading cases. Then move to primary authority: the cases and statutes that actually govern. Read the leading cases yourself rather than relying on how a textbook describes them; graders notice when your characterization of a holding comes secondhand.

As you read, capture three things for every source: the proposition it supports, the key facts or reasoning, and the pinpoint citation. If your course uses the Bluebook, record citations in the correct form as you go. Retrofitting citations at the end is where formatting errors breed. Our Bluebook citation guide covers the formats for cases, statutes, and law review articles, and the legal research paper guide goes deeper on research strategy for longer projects.

Step 3: Build a Thesis You Can Defend

For problem questions, the thesis is your overall conclusion: on balance, the plaintiff's claim succeeds. For evaluative questions, it is your position on the rule. Either way, state it in the introduction, in one or two sentences, and preview the structure that will prove it. A usable formula: "This essay argues that [position]. It first [first move], then [second move], and finally addresses [the main counterargument]." Legal readers value signposting. An introduction that maps the essay is not wasted space; it is the grader's first evidence that your analysis is organized.

Step 4: Write Body Paragraphs in IRAC

IRAC is the engine of legal analysis. Each issue in your essay gets its own IRAC cycle, and longer issues may need several. The four moves:

  1. Issue. State the precise legal question in one sentence: whether the defendant's conduct constituted a breach of the duty of care.
  2. Rule. State the governing law with authority: the elements of the test, the leading case or statute, and any relevant distinctions. Be accurate and complete here; a misstated rule contaminates everything after it.
  3. Application. Apply the rule to your facts, element by element. This is where the marks are. Use the facts the question gave you, argue both ways where the answer is genuinely contestable, and connect each fact to a legal consequence. The tell of weak analysis is a paragraph that states a rule and then jumps straight to a conclusion. The tell of strong analysis is sustained fact-to-rule matching: because X happened, element Y is satisfied, as the court found on similar facts in Z.
  4. Conclusion. State the outcome for that issue in one or two sentences, then move to the next issue.

A quick test for balance: in most essays, the application should take up at least half of each IRAC cycle. If your rule statements dwarf your application, you are describing law rather than practicing analysis.

Step 5: Handle Counterarguments Head-On

Legal questions are set because they are arguable, and graders reward students who engage the arguability. For every major conclusion, ask: what is the best argument on the other side? Present it fairly, in its strongest form, then explain why your reading prevails: the counterauthority is distinguishable on its facts, the policy concern it raises is better handled another way, or the weight of authority favors your position. An essay that concedes a small point while defending the large one reads as more credible, not less. Avoid straw-manning the opposition; a weak counterargument you knock down easily convinces no one.

Step 6: Conclude Like a Lawyer

A legal conclusion is short and decisive. Restate the question, state your answer, and compress your reasoning into two or three sentences. For problem questions, end with practical advice: the claim is likely to succeed on grounds A and B, vulnerable on C, and the client should proceed accordingly. Do not introduce new authority or new analysis in the conclusion, and do not trail off into "only time will tell." The conclusion is the last thing a grader reads; make it the confident summary of everything you have already proven.

Exam Essays Versus Take-Home Essays

The same skills apply in both settings, but the strategy shifts. In a timed exam, budget your minutes by issue count rather than polish: a complete IRAC pass on every issue beats a beautifully written analysis of half of them. Spend the first ten percent of your time reading the fact pattern twice and outlining issues in the margin, then write. In a take-home essay, the expectations invert: completeness is assumed, so precision, authority, and organization carry the grade. Verify every citation, refine your headings until they state conclusions, and use the extra time to strengthen the counterargument section, which is where most submitted essays are thinnest. In both settings, leave yourself a final read-through; five minutes of review catches the misstated rule or orphaned issue that costs a grade band.

A Checklist Before You Submit

  • Every issue the question raises has been identified and analyzed in its own IRAC cycle.
  • Every rule statement carries accurate authority with correct citations.
  • Application outweighs description in every paragraph.
  • The strongest counterargument appears and is answered.
  • The conclusion answers the question asked, not a nearby question.
  • Citations follow the required format throughout, whether Bluebook or another style your course specifies.

Law essays reward process. Decode the question, research in sequence, argue in IRAC, and respect the counterargument, and the grade follows. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the IRAC method in legal writing?

IRAC stands for Issue, Rule, Application, and Conclusion. It is the standard paragraph structure for legal analysis: state the legal issue, state the governing rule with authority, apply the rule to the specific facts, and state a concise conclusion. Most law school essays and bar exam answers are graded on how well you execute the application step.

How long should a law school essay be?

Follow your assignment or exam instructions. Take-home essays commonly run 2,000 to 4,000 words, while exam essays are constrained by time rather than length. In both cases, complete analysis of every issue earns more credit than polished prose on a single issue.

Should a law essay argue one side or discuss both?

Usually both. Legal analysis requires you to present your position, then address the strongest counterarguments and explain why your reading of the law prevails. An essay that ignores obvious counterarguments reads as one-sided and loses marks for incomplete analysis.

What citation style do law essays use?

In the United States, law schools use the Bluebook citation system. Some undergraduate legal studies courses accept APA or MLA, so check your syllabus. If Bluebook is required, cite cases, statutes, and secondary sources in footnotes according to Bluebook rules.
