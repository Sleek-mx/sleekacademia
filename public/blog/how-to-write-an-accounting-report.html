How to Write an Accounting Report: Format and Examples

A practical guide to structuring accounting reports that earn top marks

Accounting reports are a staple assessment in business, finance, and accounting programmes. Unlike general business reports, accounting reports require you to engage with financial data, apply accounting standards, and draw conclusions that are grounded in the numbers. The format is standard enough to learn as a template, yet flexible enough to adapt to different assignments.

Common Types of Accounting Reports

Your assignment brief will typically ask for one of these report types. Knowing which one you are writing helps you choose the right structure and emphasis.

Report Type Purpose Key Sections
Financial Analysis Report Evaluate a company's financial health using ratio analysis Company overview, ratio calculations, trend analysis, recommendation
Management Accounting Report Analyse costs, budgets, or performance for internal decision-making Cost analysis, variance analysis, budgeting, recommendations
Audit Report Assess the accuracy and compliance of financial statements Scope, methodology, findings, audit opinion
Case Study Report Apply accounting concepts to a specific business scenario Situation analysis, application of concepts, conclusion

The Standard Accounting Report Structure

Most accounting reports follow this sequence. Use it as your default unless your brief specifies a different format.

Executive Summary

Summarise the purpose of the report, the company or scenario analysed, the key findings from the financial data, and your main recommendations. Write this section last. For accounting reports, include the most important ratio or figure that drives your conclusion. For example: "The analysis reveals a declining current ratio from 2.1 to 1.3 over three years, indicating worsening liquidity."

Introduction and Scope

State the purpose of the report, the entity being analysed, the accounting standards applied (e.g., IFRS, US GAAP), and the period covered. Define any limitations -- for example, if you only had access to published annual reports and not internal management accounts.

Company or Scenario Overview

Provide brief context about the company: its industry, size, business model, and any recent events that affect its financial position. This section should be concise -- one or two paragraphs -- and focused on information that is relevant to the financial analysis.

Financial Analysis

This is the core of the report. Organise it by ratio category rather than by individual ratio. The common categories are:

  • Profitability: Gross profit margin, net profit margin, return on assets (ROA), return on equity (ROE)
  • Liquidity: Current ratio, quick ratio, cash conversion cycle
  • Solvency: Debt-to-equity ratio, interest coverage ratio
  • Efficiency: Inventory turnover, receivables turnover, asset turnover

For each category, present the ratios in a table, then interpret what they mean. Do not just state the numbers -- explain the trend, compare with industry benchmarks or prior periods, and discuss the implications. A current ratio of 1.5 might be healthy in retail but concerning in manufacturing, where higher liquidity is typically expected.

Discussion and Interpretation

This section draws together the findings from the financial analysis. Identify the most significant strengths and weaknesses revealed by the ratios. Discuss any contradictions -- for instance, strong profitability combined with weak liquidity -- and explain what they suggest about the company's overall position.

Conclusion and Recommendations

Summarise the main findings without introducing new information. Then make recommendations that are specific, actionable, and grounded in the analysis. A recommendation to "improve profitability" is too vague. "Reduce cost of goods sold by renegotiating supplier contracts, targeting a 2-percentage-point improvement in gross margin" is specific and traceable back to your analysis.

References and Appendices

List all sources in the required referencing style (APA 7th, Harvard, or your institution's preferred format). Appendices should include full financial statements, detailed ratio calculations, and any supporting data. Every appendix must be referenced in the body of the report.

Accounting report writing is a skill that develops with practice. By following this structure, selecting the right ratios, and focusing on interpretation rather than description, you will produce reports that demonstrate both technical competence and analytical thinking. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a financial report and an accounting report?

A financial report focuses on presenting financial statements and performance metrics for external stakeholders. An accounting report is broader and may include internal analysis, ratio calculations, cost analysis, and management recommendations.

Which accounting standards should I reference?

Most university courses teach IFRS (International Financial Reporting Standards) or the local GAAP. US-based courses typically use US GAAP. Check your assignment brief and reference the applicable standard throughout your report.

How many financial ratios should I include?

Include ratios from at least three categories: profitability, liquidity, and solvency. Four to six well-chosen ratios with clear interpretation are better than a dozen ratios with no analysis.

Should I include raw financial statements in the body or appendix?

Place full financial statements (income statement, balance sheet, cash flow statement) in an appendix and refer to them in the body. The body should contain only the extracts and ratios you are discussing.
