How to Write a Cybersecurity Analysis Paper

Threat identification, risk assessment, mitigation, and recommendations

Cybersecurity analysis papers are a common assessment type in cybersecurity, IT security, and network management courses. They require you to examine a system, organisation, or incident through a security lens: identifying threats, assessing risks, proposing mitigations, and justifying your recommendations. Unlike a general IT report, a cybersecurity analysis paper uses specialised frameworks and vocabulary. Getting the structure right is essential.

Building a Threat Model

A threat model identifies what you are protecting, what threats exist, and how they might materialise. Start by defining the system boundary. What assets are in scope? User data, network infrastructure, intellectual property, physical devices? Every asset that could be harmed by a security incident belongs in your model.

Use a recognised threat modelling framework. STRIDE (Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information Disclosure, Denial of Service, Elevation of Privilege) is common for academic work because it provides a structured lens for categorising threats. For each asset, ask which STRIDE categories apply and document the specific threat scenarios.

Present your threat model as a table with columns for asset, threat type, threat scenario, and potential impact. This makes it easy for assessors to follow your reasoning.

Performing a Risk Assessment

Risk assessment evaluates the likelihood and impact of each identified threat. The standard approach is a risk matrix: plot each threat on a grid where one axis represents likelihood (rare, unlikely, possible, likely, almost certain) and the other represents impact (insignificant, minor, moderate, major, catastrophic).

Likelihood \ Impact Insignificant Minor Moderate Major Catastrophic
Almost Certain Medium High High Critical Critical
Likely Medium Medium High High Critical
Possible Low Medium Medium High High
Unlikely Low Low Medium Medium High
Rare Low Low Low Medium Medium

For each threat in your model, assign a likelihood and impact rating, then identify the overall risk level. Justify your ratings with evidence where possible. For example: "The risk of a phishing attack is rated Likely / Major because the organisation has 500 employees, no multi-factor authentication, and the sector is a common target for credential theft."

Developing Mitigation Strategies

Every risk above the acceptable threshold needs a mitigation strategy. Organise mitigations by category: preventive controls (firewalls, access controls, encryption), detective controls (logging, monitoring, intrusion detection), and corrective controls (backup, incident response plan, patch management).

For each mitigation, explain how it reduces the risk. A strong mitigation section does not just list technologies -- it shows that you understand the trade-offs. A firewall reduces external threats but does not protect against insider threats. Encryption protects data at rest but adds management overhead. Acknowledging limitations demonstrates critical thinking.

Structuring the Case Study

Most cybersecurity analysis assignments include a case study component. The case study applies your analytical framework to a real or hypothetical scenario. A well-structured case study follows this sequence:

  • Background: Describe the organisation or system, its assets, and its operating context.
  • Identified threats: Apply your threat model to the specific case. Which threats are most relevant?
  • Risk assessment: Use the risk matrix to prioritise threats. Show your working.
  • Mitigations: Propose controls for the highest-priority risks. Justify cost and effort where relevant.
  • Residual risk: Acknowledge what remains after mitigations. No system is perfectly secure.

When using a real-world incident as your case study, cite your sources thoroughly. Information security incidents are well documented in breach reports, regulatory filings, and post-mortem analyses. Use these as primary sources rather than relying on news summaries.

Writing the Conclusion and Recommendations

Your conclusion should synthesise the key risks and mitigations without introducing new information. The recommendations section that follows should prioritise actions based on the risk assessment. Format recommendations as actionable statements: "Implement multi-factor authentication for all administrative accounts within 90 days," rather than "Improve authentication security."

Cybersecurity analysis is as much about clear communication as it is about technical knowledge. By following a structured approach with a threat model, risk matrix, and prioritised mitigations, you will produce a paper that demonstrates both analytical depth and practical understanding. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a threat and a vulnerability?

A threat is a potential cause of harm, such as a hacker or a malware strain. A vulnerability is a weakness that a threat could exploit, such as an unpatched server or a weak password policy.

How do I choose a framework for a cybersecurity analysis?

Common academic frameworks include NIST CSF, ISO 27001, and MITRE ATT&CK. Choose the one that best aligns with your assignment scope. NIST CSF is widely used for organisational analysis, while MITRE ATT&CK is preferred for threat-centric analyses.

What should a risk matrix look like?

A standard risk matrix has likelihood on one axis and impact on the other, creating a 5x5 or 3x3 grid. Risks are categorised as low, medium, high, or critical based on their position in the grid.

Do I need to include a real-world case study?

Most cybersecurity analysis assignments expect a case study component. If your brief does not specify one, consider using a well-documented incident such as the SolarWinds breach, Colonial Pipeline attack, or a data breach from a major organisation.
