Bluebook Citation Guide for Law Students

Cases, statutes, law review articles, books, signals, and short forms with worked examples

July 2026 • 10 min read

By the Sleek Academia tutoring team

menu_bookPart of our Assignment Help for Law Students →

The Bluebook has a reputation for being needlessly complicated, and some of that reputation is earned. But the core of it is a small set of formats repeated thousands of times: cases, statutes, law review articles, and books, plus a short list of signals and short forms. Learn those formats cold and you will handle ninety percent of every citation you ever write. The remaining ten percent is what the manual's index is for.

This guide gives you the working formats with examples you can pattern-match against, the signals you will actually use, and the short forms that keep your footnotes clean. It assumes footnote citation, which is the convention for law reviews and most academic legal writing.

Key Takeaways

  • Four source types cover most citations: cases, statutes, law review articles, and books.
  • Every borrowed proposition needs a pinpoint citation to the exact page.
  • Signals (see, see also, cf., contra) tell the reader how the source relates to your claim.
  • Id. and supra keep repeat citations short, but each has strict conditions for use.
  • Build citations correctly at the research stage, not in a final formatting marathon.

The Ground Rules

Three conventions apply everywhere before any specific format. First, typeface: case names are italicized (or underlined, in typewritten work), both in the text and in footnotes. Second, placement: academic legal writing cites in footnotes at the bottom of the page, with the footnote number following the punctuation of the sentence it supports. Third, pinpoints: when you cite a source for a specific proposition, you cite the page where that proposition appears, not just the first page of the source. A citation without a pinpoint forces the reader to hunt, and graders treat it as incomplete.

Citing Cases

The full citation for a case has five components in order: the case name, the volume, the reporter abbreviation, the first page, and the court and year in parentheses. The pattern:

  • Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483 (1954).
  • With a pinpoint: Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954).

Notes that trip students up. The case name is italicized and abbreviated according to the Bluebook's abbreviation tables (for example, Association becomes Ass'n, University becomes Univ.). The U.S. Reports citation needs no court name in the parentheses because the reporter tells you it is the Supreme Court; for any other reporter, include the court: Riggs v. Palmer, 22 N.E. 188 (N.Y. 1889). When you refer to a case in the body of your text, the name is still italicized, and the full citation typically moves to a footnote at first mention.

Citing Statutes and Constitutions

Statute citations follow the code structure: title number, code abbreviation, section symbol, and year of the code edition in parentheses:

  • Federal statute: 42 U.S.C. § 1983 (2024).
  • State statute: Cal. Civ. Code § 1542 (West 2025).
  • Constitution: U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1.

Always cite the current official code where possible, cite the year of the code volume you actually consulted (not the year the statute passed), and use the section symbol (§) rather than writing out "section." When you cite consecutive sections, use two symbols: §§ 1981-1983.

Citing Law Review Articles

Law review articles are the secondary source you will cite most, and the format has five parts: author, title, volume, journal abbreviation, first page, pinpoint, and year:

  • Guido Calabresi, Some Thoughts on Risk Distribution and the Law of Torts, 70 Yale L.J. 499, 515 (1961).

The article title is italicized, the journal name uses the Bluebook's journal abbreviations and appears in large and small capitals in typeset work (plain roman type is acceptable in student papers), and the year is the year of publication, not the volume year if they differ. For an article with multiple authors, list them in the order they appear on the piece.

Citing Books

Book citations list author, title, edition if not the first, pinpoint page, publisher, and year:

  • Richard A. Posner, Economic Analysis of Law 45 (9th ed. 2014).

The title is italicized and appears in large and small capitals in typeset work. Include the publisher only when needed for clarity or when your instructions require it; under current Bluebook practice for student work, author, title, edition, pinpoint, and year usually suffice.

Signals: Telling the Reader How the Source Relates

Signals are the small words before a citation that carry real meaning, and using the wrong one misrepresents your authority:

SignalMeaningUse When
(no signal)Direct supportThe authority states your proposition directly
SeeIndirect supportThe authority supports but does not directly state your proposition
See alsoAdditional supportYou have already cited direct support and are adding cumulative authority
Cf.Analogous supportThe authority supports a different but analogous proposition
ContraDirect contradictionThe authority directly states the contrary of your proposition
But seeContrary authorityThe authority supports a position contrary to yours and you are acknowledging it

Signals are italicized as part of the citation. The most common student error is using see for everything, including sources that state the proposition outright and need no signal. Match the signal to the relationship; it is a small detail that experienced readers notice immediately.

Short Forms: Id. and Supra

Repeat citations use short forms, and each has strict conditions:

  • Id. refers to the immediately preceding authority, but only when that authority was the sole source in the previous citation. Use id. alone for the same page and id. at 512. for a different page. If the previous footnote cited two sources, id. is unavailable and you need a short form instead.
  • Case short form. After the first full citation, use the first party's name (or a distinctive shorter name), volume, reporter, first page, "at," and pinpoint: Brown, 347 U.S. at 495.
  • Supra. For books and articles (not cases or statutes), use the author's surname, supra, and the page: Calabresi, supra note 12, at 515. Some instructors want the note number included; follow your course convention.

Common Bluebook Mistakes to Check Before Submission

  • Case names not italicized, or reporter abbreviations invented rather than looked up.
  • Missing pinpoints on citations offered for specific propositions.
  • Id. used where the previous note cited multiple sources.
  • The wrong year: the code edition year for statutes, the decision year for cases.
  • Signals used inconsistently, or see sprayed over every citation.
  • Mixing citation conventions: footnotes for some sources, in-text parentheticals for others.

The efficient habit is to build each citation correctly the first time you record a source, while the reporter volume is literally in front of you. Reconstructing citations at the revision stage is where most errors are born. Frequently Asked Questions

What is a pinpoint citation in Bluebook format?

A pinpoint citation identifies the exact page (or paragraph) where the cited proposition appears, rather than the first page of the source. In practice: cite the first page of the source, then a comma, then the pinpoint page, as in 347 U.S. 483, 495. Every proposition you borrow needs a pinpoint so the reader can verify it.

When do I use id. in a Bluebook citation?

Id. refers to the immediately preceding authority in the same footnote or the immediately preceding footnote, when that authority is the only one cited there. Use id. alone when the page is the same, or id. at [page] when citing a different page of the same source. Never use id. to refer to a source cited alongside others in the previous note.

What is the difference between see and cf. signals?

See indicates that the cited authority supports your proposition but does not state it directly. Cf. means the cited authority supports a proposition different from yours, but it is analogous enough to lend support. When the authority states your proposition directly, use no signal at all.

Do I cite in footnotes or in the text for Bluebook style?

In law reviews and most law school research papers, citations go in footnotes at the bottom of the page, not in a reference list or in-text parentheticals. Practitioners' documents such as briefs and memos cite in the body text. Your course instructions will specify which convention to follow; default to footnotes for academic papers.
