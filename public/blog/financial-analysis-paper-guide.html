Financial Analysis Paper Guide for College Students

Company selection, ratio analysis, valuation, and investment recommendations

July 2026 • 11 min read

By the Sleek Academia tutoring team

menu_bookPart of our Finance Assignment Help Guide →

Key Takeaways

  • check_circle Choose a public company with at least three years of accessible financial data.
  • check_circle Analyse ratios in categories: profitability, liquidity, solvency, and efficiency.
  • check_circle Include trend analysis -- a single year's ratios tell you little without context.
  • check_circle Your investment recommendation must be supported by the evidence you present.

A financial analysis paper is one of the most common and most demanding assignments in finance courses. It requires you to select a company, gather its financial data, calculate and interpret ratios, perform a valuation, and make an investment recommendation. The assignment tests both your technical skills and your ability to communicate financial insights clearly.

Choosing a Company

Your choice of company shapes the entire paper. Pick a company that is publicly traded (so financial statements are available), operates in an industry you understand, and has at least three years of financial data. Well-known companies like Apple, Microsoft, Nike, or Coca-Cola are good choices because extensive analyst coverage gives you benchmarks and context. Avoid companies that are newly listed, undergoing restructuring, or in complex industries like banking or insurance unless you have specific expertise.

Company Overview

Provide a brief profile of the company: its business model, revenue sources, key markets, competitive position, and recent strategic developments. Keep this section concise -- one to two paragraphs. Focus on information that is relevant to the financial analysis. For example, if the company recently acquired a major competitor, note that because it affects the financial statements and ratios.

Financial Ratio Analysis

Ratio analysis is the heart of the paper. Organise your ratios into categories and present them in a clear table before interpreting them.

Category Ratio Formula What It Measures
Profitability Gross Profit Margin Gross Profit ÷ Revenue How efficiently the company produces its goods
Profitability Net Profit Margin Net Income ÷ Revenue How much profit remains after all expenses
Profitability Return on Equity (ROE) Net Income ÷ Shareholders' Equity Return generated on shareholders' investment
Liquidity Current Ratio Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities Ability to pay short-term obligations
Liquidity Quick Ratio (Current Assets - Inventory) ÷ Current Liabilities Ability to pay short-term obligations without selling inventory
Solvency Debt-to-Equity Ratio Total Liabilities ÷ Shareholders' Equity How the company finances its operations (debt vs equity)
Efficiency Inventory Turnover COGS ÷ Average Inventory How quickly inventory is sold

For each ratio, calculate the value for each year in your analysis period and note the trend. Then interpret: is an increasing current ratio a sign of improving liquidity or inefficient use of cash? A debt-to-equity ratio of 0.8 might be conservative in a stable industry but aggressive in a cyclical one. Compare your findings with industry averages or competitor data if available.

Trend Analysis

A single year's ratios are nearly meaningless without context. Trend analysis examines how the ratios change over time and what those changes suggest. A declining gross margin might indicate rising input costs, pricing pressure, or a shift in product mix. Identify the most significant trends and explain their likely causes. Link your explanation to real events where possible -- did the company face a supply chain disruption? Did it enter a new market?

Valuation

Most financial analysis papers require at least one valuation method. Discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis is the most common. At a minimum, you should estimate the company's cost of capital (WACC), project free cash flows for 3-5 years, calculate a terminal value, and discount these to arrive at an enterprise value and implied share price.

Comparable company analysis (trading multiples) is a simpler alternative or supplement. Compare the company's P/E, EV/EBITDA, and P/B multiples with those of similar companies. If your company trades at a discount to peers, is there a reason, or does it represent an opportunity?

Investment Recommendation

State your recommendation clearly: buy, sell, or hold. Support it with the evidence from your ratio analysis, trend analysis, and valuation. A buy recommendation should be based on specific strengths such as improving profitability, strong liquidity, or an undervalued share price. A sell recommendation might be justified by deteriorating margins, rising debt levels, or an overvalued share price.

Acknowledge risks and uncertainties. No recommendation is risk-free, and acknowledging the limitations of your analysis demonstrates maturity. If your DCF valuation is highly sensitive to the assumed growth rate, say so.

Financial analysis is both a technical skill and a narrative one. The numbers provide the evidence; your job is to tell the story they reveal. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best company to analyse for a financial analysis paper?

Choose a publicly traded company with at least three years of published financial statements. Good options include well-known firms like Apple, Microsoft, Nike, or Coca-Cola. Avoid very new companies or those with complex structures that are hard to analyse.

What is the difference between DCF and comparable company analysis?

DCF (discounted cash flow) values a company based on its projected future cash flows. Comparable company analysis values it by comparing its valuation multiples (P/E, EV/EBITDA) to similar publicly traded companies. Many papers include both.

How many years of data should I include?

Three to five years of historical financial data is standard for a financial analysis paper. This is enough to identify trends without overwhelming the analysis.

Do I need to include a valuation model?

Most financial analysis papers at the undergraduate level require at least one valuation approach. DCF is the most common. If your course has not covered valuation, check with your lecturer -- a recommendation based on ratio analysis alone may be sufficient.
