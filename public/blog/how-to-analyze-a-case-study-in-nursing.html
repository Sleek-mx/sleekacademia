How to Analyze a Case Study in Nursing

A step-by-step framework from assessment to evaluation, built on the nursing process

July 2026 • 10 min read

By the Sleek Academia tutoring team

menu_bookPart of our Assignment Help for Nursing Students →

The nursing case study is the closest thing school gives you to real clinical reasoning. You are handed a patient, their history, their vitals, their labs, and their story, and asked to think like the nurse responsible for them: what is happening, what matters most, what will you do, and how will you know it worked. Students who treat it as a reading comprehension exercise write weak analyses. Students who treat it as a shift handoff write strong ones.

The framework you already know applies directly: the nursing process, ADPIE. Assessment, Diagnosis, Planning, Implementation, Evaluation. This guide shows you how to run a written case analysis through each phase, with the formats and phrasing instructors expect.

Key Takeaways

  • Read the case twice: once for the story, once with a pen to extract every abnormal finding.
  • Cluster your assessment data into patterns before choosing diagnoses.
  • Write NANDA diagnoses in the three-part format, each traceable to case data.
  • Set SMART goals and pair every intervention with an evidence-based rationale.
  • Evaluate against the goal explicitly: met, partially met, or not met, with next steps.

Step 1: Read the Case Like a Nurse, Not a Student

Read the case twice. The first read is for the story: who is this patient, why are they here, what is the trajectory. The second read is extraction. With a pen or a blank document, pull out every clinically significant finding: abnormal vitals, abnormal labs, relevant history, medications, allergies, psychosocial factors, functional status, and anything the case flags as a change from baseline.

Two habits separate strong analysts here. First, note what is missing as well as what is present. A case that gives you a postoperative patient with no mention of pain assessment is inviting you to identify that gap. Second, distinguish relevant from incidental data. Not every detail in the case is a cue. Part of clinical judgment, and part of your grade, is showing you know which findings matter and why.

Step 2: Build the Assessment Picture

Organize your extracted data before you diagnose anything. Group findings into clusters that point in the same direction. For a patient with pneumonia, one cluster might be respiratory: SpO2 of 88 percent, respiratory rate 28, productive cough, crackles in the right lower lobe, fever of 38.9. Another cluster might be functional: fatigue, inability to complete activities of daily living, poor oral intake.

As you cluster, separate subjective data from objective data. Subjective data is what the patient reports: pain ratings, nausea, shortness of breath, anxiety. Objective data is what you observe or measure: vital signs, lab values, physical findings, observed behaviors. NANDA defining characteristics draw on both, and many rubrics ask you to label them, so build the habit of tagging each finding as you extract it.

Present the assessment section of your paper as organized prose or a structured summary: demographics and chief complaint, history, current medications, physical findings organized by system, and diagnostics with abnormal values flagged. Reference ranges help here. Writing "potassium 2.9 (normal 3.5 to 5.0)" shows you know the finding is abnormal without making the reader look it up.

Step 3: Formulate NANDA Nursing Diagnoses

From your clusters, select your nursing diagnoses and write them in the standard three-part format: the diagnosis label, related to the etiology, as evidenced by the defining characteristics. The format is not decorative. Each part does a job:

  • The label names the human response: Impaired Gas Exchange, Acute Pain, Activity Intolerance, Risk for Falls.
  • The related-to statement identifies the cause you can influence: related to alveolar-capillary membrane changes, related to surgical tissue trauma.
  • The as-evidenced-by statement anchors the diagnosis in your data: as evidenced by SpO2 of 88 percent on room air, respiratory rate of 28, and patient report of dyspnea at rest.

Then prioritize. Rank your diagnoses using the ABCs and Maslow's hierarchy: physiological and safety needs before psychosocial ones. A risk diagnosis never outranks an actual gas exchange problem. Most assignments want three to five diagnoses, developed in depth. If the underlying disease process is unclear to you, work through it the way our pathophysiology paper guide describes, because a diagnosis you cannot connect to the mechanism is a diagnosis you cannot defend.

Step 4: Plan Goals That Can Be Measured

For each priority diagnosis, write goals in SMART form: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound. The patient, not the nurse, is the subject of the goal. Compare these two:

  • Weak: "Improve oxygenation."
  • SMART: "The patient will maintain SpO2 at or above 92 percent on room air within 48 hours."

Include at least one short-term goal (hours to days) and one long-term goal (by discharge or beyond) for your top diagnosis if the rubric asks. Every goal must be checkable, meaning that at evaluation time you can look at data and say whether it was met.

Step 5: Choose Interventions With Rationales

Interventions are the nursing actions that move the patient toward the goal. For each diagnosis, three to five interventions is typical. The critical rule: every intervention needs a rationale, and the rationale needs a source. The structure to reuse is a simple table:

InterventionRationale (with citation)
Position the patient in high Fowler's positionUpright positioning promotes lung expansion and improves ventilation-perfusion matching (Ackley et al., 2023)
Administer oxygen per prescribed titration protocolSupplemental oxygen corrects hypoxemia and reduces work of breathing (American Thoracic Society, 2024)
Encourage fluid intake of 2 liters daily unless contraindicatedAdequate hydration thins secretions and supports expectoration (Ackley et al., 2023)
Monitor respiratory status and SpO2 every four hoursSerial monitoring detects deterioration early, when intervention is most effective (American Thoracic Society, 2024)

Mark independent interventions (within nursing scope, like positioning and teaching) separately from dependent ones (requiring a provider order, like oxygen titration or medications) if your rubric distinguishes them. And if the case centers on a medication problem, the same mechanism-first reasoning from our pharmacology paper guide applies to your medication-related interventions.

Step 6: Evaluate Against the Goals

Evaluation closes the loop, and skipping it is one of the most common reasons case analyses lose their final marks. For each goal, state explicitly whether it was met, partially met, or not met, using the case's follow-up data where provided. Then state the clinical consequence: continue the plan, modify the intervention, or revise the goal. "Goal partially met: SpO2 reached 91 percent on 2 liters of oxygen at 48 hours. Continue current interventions and reassess in 24 hours; notify provider if no improvement by 72 hours" reads like a nurse thinking forward, which is exactly the competency being graded.

Where the case gives no follow-up data, say so and project the evaluation instead: state what findings at the next assessment would indicate the goal was met, and what findings would trigger escalation. An evaluation section that names its own data source, whether case follow-up or projected reassessment, shows the marker you understand evaluation as a clinical act rather than a formality.

A Structure You Can Reuse for Any Case

  1. Case overview: patient, setting, chief complaint, trajectory
  2. Assessment: organized findings by cluster or system, with abnormal values flagged
  3. Nursing diagnoses: three to five, three-part format, prioritized
  4. Planning: SMART goals, short-term and long-term
  5. Interventions: three to five per priority diagnosis, each with a cited rationale
  6. Evaluation: met, partially met, or not met, with next steps
  7. Conclusion: the key clinical lesson the case teaches

Support your rationales and any pathophysiology statements with current sources in APA format; the nursing APA guide covers the formatting if you need it. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the correct format for a NANDA nursing diagnosis?

The standard three-part format is: the diagnosis label, related to the etiology or cause, as evidenced by the defining characteristics from your assessment data. For example: Impaired Gas Exchange related to alveolar-capillary membrane changes as evidenced by SpO2 of 88 percent on room air and dyspnea on exertion. Every part must be traceable to data in the case.

How many nursing diagnoses should a case study analysis include?

Follow your rubric, but three to five prioritized diagnoses is typical. Rank them by urgency using ABCs (airway, breathing, circulation) and Maslow's hierarchy. One thoroughly developed diagnosis with goals, interventions, and evaluation scores better than a long list of undeveloped ones.

What is the difference between a goal and an intervention in a care plan?

A goal is the patient outcome you are aiming for, written in SMART form: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound, such as the patient will maintain SpO2 above 92 percent within 24 hours. An intervention is a nursing action performed to reach that goal, such as positioning the patient in high Fowler's or administering prescribed oxygen.

Do nursing case study papers need citations?

Usually yes. While the case data itself needs no citation, your rationales for interventions should be supported by evidence from textbooks, peer-reviewed journals, or clinical guidelines, cited in APA format. Many rubrics explicitly require a citation for each intervention's rationale.
