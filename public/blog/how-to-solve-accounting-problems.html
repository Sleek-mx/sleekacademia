How to Solve Accounting Problems Step-by-Step

A reliable framework for journal entries, T-accounts, and trial balances

Accounting problems can feel intimidating, especially when a question presents you with a list of transactions and asks for journal entries, ledger accounts, and a trial balance. The key is not to memorise every possible scenario but to learn a repeatable process that works for any problem. Once you have the framework, you can apply it to introductory, intermediate, and even advanced accounting questions.

The Five-Step Accounting Problem Framework

Step Action What You Produce
1. Read and Identify Read the problem, identify each transaction and the accounts affected List of transactions with accounts
2. Classify Determine if each account is an asset, liability, equity, revenue, or expense Account classification table
3. Apply Debit/Credit Rules Determine which account is debited and which is credited for each transaction Journal entries
4. Post and Calculate Post entries to T-accounts and calculate ending balances T-accounts or ledger
5. Verify and Present Prepare a trial balance and verify that debits equal credits Trial balance

Step 1: Read and Identify Transactions

Read the problem carefully. Every transaction involves at least two accounts. List each transaction and the accounts it touches. For example, if the problem says "Purchased inventory on credit for $5,000," the accounts are Inventory and Accounts Payable. Underline or highlight the monetary amounts and note whether they are incoming or outgoing.

Step 2: Classify Each Account

Before you can apply debits and credits, you need to know what type of account you are dealing with. The five account types are:

  • Assets: Cash, Accounts Receivable, Inventory, Equipment, Buildings
  • Liabilities: Accounts Payable, Notes Payable, Unearned Revenue
  • Equity: Common Stock, Retained Earnings, Owner's Capital
  • Revenue: Sales Revenue, Service Revenue, Interest Income
  • Expenses: Cost of Goods Sold, Rent Expense, Salaries Expense, Depreciation

If you are unsure whether an account is an expense or an asset, ask: does this provide future economic benefit? If yes, it is likely an asset. If it is consumed in the current period, it is an expense.

Step 3: Apply the Rules of Debits and Credits

The rules are derived from the accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Equity. The corollary for debits and credits is:

  • Assets increase with debits, decrease with credits.
  • Liabilities increase with credits, decrease with debits.
  • Equity increases with credits, decreases with debits.
  • Revenue increases equity, so revenue increases with credits.
  • Expenses decrease equity, so expenses increase with debits.

For each transaction, determine which account receives the debit and which receives the credit. Every journal entry must have at least one debit and one credit, and total debits must equal total credits.

Step 4: Post to T-Accounts

A T-account is a visual representation of a ledger account. The account name goes at the top, debits are recorded on the left side, and credits on the right side. After posting all entries, calculate the ending balance for each account.

T-accounts serve two purposes: they help you see the balance of each account at a glance, and they make it easier to spot errors. If an asset account has a credit balance, something is wrong -- assets should normally have debit balances.

Step 5: Prepare and Verify the Trial Balance

The trial balance lists every account with its ending debit or credit balance. Total debits must equal total credits. If they do not, check for these common errors:

  • A journal entry was posted as a debit when it should have been a credit, or vice versa.
  • A debit or credit was omitted entirely.
  • A calculation error occurred when balancing a T-account.
  • The wrong amount was posted.

If the difference between total debits and total credits is divisible by 2, you may have posted a debit as a credit (or vice versa). If it is divisible by 9, you may have transposed digits (e.g., 540 entered as 450).

Worked Example: Journal Entries to Trial Balance

Here is a simple illustration. Transaction: "The owner invested $10,000 cash into the business."

  • Step 1: Accounts affected: Cash and Owner's Capital.
  • Step 2: Cash is an asset. Owner's Capital is equity.
  • Step 3: Assets increase with debits, so debit Cash $10,000. Equity increases with credits, so credit Owner's Capital $10,000.
  • Step 4: Post to T-accounts. Cash has a debit balance of $10,000. Owner's Capital has a credit balance of $10,000.
  • Step 5: Trial balance: Cash (debit $10,000), Owner's Capital (credit $10,000). Total debits = $10,000. Total credits = $10,000. Balanced.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most common mistake students make in accounting problems?

The most common mistake is misclassifying accounts. Students confuse expenses with assets, or liabilities with equity. Learning the account types -- assets, liabilities, equity, revenue, and expenses -- is the foundation of solving any accounting problem correctly.

What is the easiest way to remember debits and credits?

Use the accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Assets increase with debits and decrease with credits. Liabilities and equity increase with credits and decrease with debits. Revenue increases equity (credit), expenses decrease equity (debit).

How do I know if a trial balance is correct?

A trial balance is correct when total debits equal total credits. If they do not balance, check for: calculation errors, a debit entered as a credit (or vice versa), a missing entry, or an entry posted to the wrong account.

Should I show T-accounts in my assignment?

Yes. Most accounting lecturers expect to see T-accounts or ledger accounts because they show the flow of transactions through each account. They also make it easier to identify errors when the trial balance does not add up.
