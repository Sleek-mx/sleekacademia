How to Write a Literature Review: A Student Guide

Structure, synthesis, and the difference between summarizing sources and actually using them

July 2026 • 10 min read

By the Sleek Academia tutoring team

menu_bookPart of a bigger paper? See the complete research paper guide →

The literature review is where good students produce their worst writing. Not because they cannot read, but because nobody explains what the document is actually for. They deliver a parade of summaries — Smith found this, Jones found that, Lee concluded something else — and receive the same margin comment every time: this is an annotated bibliography, not a literature review. The fix is a single idea: a literature review does not describe sources. It builds an argument out of them.

What a Literature Review Actually Does

Whether it stands alone as an assignment or sits inside a research paper, thesis, or capstone, a literature review answers four questions for the reader:

  • What is known about this topic, established by converging evidence?
  • What is debated — where do credible studies disagree, and why?
  • How did we get here — which foundational studies and theories shaped the field?
  • What is missing — the gap your paper, project, or study will address?

That last question is the point of the exercise. A literature review exists to justify new work: your research question, your thesis, your project. Every paragraph should move the reader toward the conclusion that this gap matters and someone should fill it — which is exactly the position your own paper then steps into. If you are writing the review as a section of a longer paper, our research paper guide shows where it fits in the full structure.

Narrative vs. Systematic: Know Which One You Are Writing

Two review types get confused constantly. A systematic review follows a formal, reproducible protocol — registered search strings, documented inclusion criteria, PRISMA flow diagrams, often a meta-analysis of pooled results. It is a research method in itself and takes months. A narrative (or traditional) review is what nearly every course assignment actually asks for: a critical, thematic synthesis of the literature relevant to your question, written in pages, not months. Check the brief before you begin. If your instructor wants a systematic review, the expectations and timeline are entirely different, and the assignment will say so explicitly. Everything in this guide applies to the narrative kind.

Step 1: Define the Scope Before You Search

Unscoped reading is how a six-page review becomes a three-week spiral. Before opening a database, write down three limits:

  • The research question. Not a topic — a question. "Nurse burnout" is a topic. "Which organizational interventions reduce burnout among medical-surgical nurses?" is a question, and it tells you exactly which articles belong in your review.
  • Inclusion criteria. Publication window (commonly the last five years), study types (peer-reviewed empirical studies, systematic reviews), population, and setting. Write them down and apply them consistently.
  • The gap hypothesis. Your early sense of what the literature has not answered. It will sharpen as you read, but having one keeps you reading with purpose.

Step 2: Search Strategically, Not Exhaustively

Good searching is iterative. Start in the databases your field actually uses — CINAHL and PubMed for nursing and health, ERIC for education, PsycINFO for psychology, HeinOnline for law, Scopus or Google Scholar broadly. Build a search string from your question's key concepts joined with AND, and synonyms joined with OR: (nurse OR nursing staff) AND (burnout OR compassion fatigue) AND (intervention OR program).

Then work the citations in both directions. When you find one strong, recent article, mine its reference list for the foundational studies (backward chaining) and use the database's "cited by" feature to find everything published since (forward chaining). Two or three strong seed articles, chained properly, usually surface 80 percent of the relevant literature. Save everything into a reference manager — Zotero is free and exports a formatted bibliography in seconds. Whatever citation style your course requires, keep the details clean from the start; our APA 7 guide covers the most common format.

Step 3: Take Notes Toward Themes, Not Summaries

The way you annotate determines whether the matrix fills easily or not at all. For each article, record five things in your own words: the research question, the method and sample, the two or three findings that matter, the limitations the authors admit, and — most importantly — the theme or variable this article speaks to in your review. That last field is what turns a pile of summaries into material for synthesis. If a note cannot name the theme it belongs to, either the article is off-scope or your theme list needs a new column. Annotate in your reference manager or a single running document, and copy the full citation into the note before anything else, so no source is ever orphaned from its details.

While you read, weigh the evidence, not just the findings. In health and social science fields, a rough hierarchy runs from expert opinion at the bottom up through single qualitative studies, cohort studies, randomized controlled trials, and systematic reviews at the top. A finding from a 40-person pilot study and a finding from a 12-site randomized trial do not enter your review with the same force, and saying so in a clause — "in a small single-site study" versus "in a large multisite trial" — is the easiest critical-commentary mark you will ever earn.

Step 4: Screen and Organize With a Synthesis Matrix

Reading for a review is not reading for comprehension — it is reading for comparison. The tool that changes everything is the synthesis matrix: a table where each row is a source and each column is a theme, variable, or finding you care about.

SourceMethodStaffing findingLeadership finding
Aiken et al. (2023)Cross-sectional surveyLower ratios linked to lower burnoutNot measured
Dall'Ora et al. (2024)Systematic reviewMixed staffing evidenceSupportive leadership buffers burnout
Nguyen et al. (2025)RCTNo effect of float poolsUnit manager training cut intent to leave

Fill the matrix as you read, and the structure of your review reveals itself: the columns with rich, contradictory entries are your themes. The empty cells are your gaps. Students who skip the matrix end up organizing their review by source — one paragraph per article — which is the annotated bibliography trap all over again.

Step 5: Choose a Structure That Fits Your Argument

Four structures dominate, and the right choice depends on what your literature actually shows:

StructureBest whenWatch out for
ThematicThe literature clusters around recurring issues or debatesThemes that are really just subtopics, with no argument connecting them
ChronologicalThe field's thinking has clearly evolved over timeDrifting into a history lesson instead of an argument
MethodologicalFindings differ because methods differ (qualitative vs. quantitative)Discussing methods without connecting them to findings
TheoreticalCompeting frameworks explain the same phenomenonDescribing theories without weighing their evidence

For most student reviews, thematic is the safest and strongest choice, because it forces synthesis by design. You can blend structures — thematic overall, chronological within a theme — but make one of them dominant so the reader always knows where they are.

Step 6: Write the Review in Three Movements

The introduction

Open by establishing the topic's importance, state your scope (what is in, what is out, and why), name your organizing structure, and preview the gap you will land on. Four or five sentences can do all of this. Do not open with a source — your first paragraph should be the only one in the review that could survive without a citation, because it frames the conversation rather than joining it.

The body: synthesis paragraphs

Each body paragraph makes one claim about the literature — not about a source. The shape is: topic sentence stating the claim, evidence from multiple sources agreeing or disagreeing, and your analysis of what the pattern means. Watch the difference:

  • Summary (weak): "Aiken et al. (2023) surveyed nurses and found lower staffing ratios associated with less burnout. Nguyen et al. (2025) ran a trial of float pools and found no effect."
  • Synthesis (strong): "Evidence for staffing interventions is weaker than its popularity suggests. While survey data link lower ratios to reduced burnout (Aiken et al., 2023), the only randomized trial of a staffing intervention found no effect (Nguyen et al., 2025), and a systematic review judged the overall evidence mixed (Dall'Ora et al., 2024). The pattern suggests staffing may matter less directly than the leadership environment mediating it."

Same sources, completely different document. The strong version compares, weighs, and points somewhere. Here is the same principle on the disagreement side: "Where the literature splits, the split tracks the method. Qualitative studies describe staffing interventions as welcome but insufficient (Lee & Park, 2024; Osei, 2025), while the quantitative trials find measurable but modest effects (Nguyen et al., 2025). Read together, the two bodies of work suggest the intervention works only when the unit culture supports it — a hypothesis none of the reviewed studies tests directly." Notice what happened: the disagreement became the gap. That move — tension into research opening — is the most valuable sentence pattern in the form. Keep a short list of synthesis verbs at hand: converge, diverge, corroborate, contradict, extend, qualify, complicate. If every sentence says "found" or "stated," you are summarizing.

The conclusion

Close by stating what the literature collectively establishes, what remains contested, and the specific gap — then connect that gap directly to your own study, thesis, or project. The reader should finish your review believing your research question is not just interesting but necessary.

The hand-off sentence matters more than students expect. A formula that works: "Taken together, these findings establish X, but they leave Y unexamined — the question this paper takes up." One sentence, and your review has done its full job: mapped the field, earned the reader's trust, and made your own work feel inevitable rather than arbitrary. If the review is a standalone assignment rather than a paper section, end one step earlier — with the gap named and its significance argued — since there is no study of your own to introduce.

A One-Week Literature Review Workflow

For a standard eight-source course review, this schedule holds:

  • Day 1: Scope — research question, inclusion criteria, gap hypothesis. Thirty minutes in the databases to confirm the topic is researchable.
  • Days 2–3: Search and screen — seed articles, citation chaining, abstracts read against your criteria, sources saved into a reference manager.
  • Days 4–5: Read and fill the synthesis matrix, one article at a time, noting themes as you go.
  • Day 6: Draft — themes become sections, matrix rows become synthesized paragraphs, gap lands in the conclusion.
  • Day 7: Revise for synthesis (every paragraph citing multiple sources), then format citations and references.

The Mistakes That Mark You as a Beginner

  • One source per paragraph. The defining symptom of an annotated bibliography. Almost every paragraph should cite at least two or three sources making a shared point.
  • Only agreeing sources. A review without disagreement reads as cherry-picked. The contested areas are where your analysis earns its marks.
  • Quotations everywhere. Reviews are built on paraphrase. Quote only when the original wording itself matters, and cite every paraphrase properly — see the APA examples if you are unsure.
  • No critical voice. Note sample sizes, methods, and limitations where they matter. "A small qualitative study" and "a large multisite trial" should not carry equal weight in your argument.
  • A conclusion that just restates. If your conclusion does not name a gap and connect it to your own work, the review has not done its job.

The literature review is a learnable form: scope, search, matrix, structure, synthesis. Once the matrix is full, the writing is honestly the easy part. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a literature review and an annotated bibliography?

An annotated bibliography summarizes sources one by one in separate entries. A literature review weaves sources together around themes, comparing and contrasting them to show what the field knows, debates, and has missed.

How many sources should a literature review have?

Undergraduate reviews typically synthesize 8 to 15 sources; graduate and thesis chapters often use 20 to 50 or more. The assignment brief rules, and depth of synthesis matters more than raw count.

How long should a literature review be?

As a standalone assignment, four to eight pages is common. As a section of a research paper, the review usually runs 20 to 30 percent of the total length. Follow the brief where one is given.

Can I include older sources in a literature review?

Yes, deliberately. Seminal studies and foundational theories belong regardless of age. Most instructors expect the majority of sources to be current, often within the last five years, with older works included because they shaped the field.

What does it mean to synthesize sources?

Synthesis means putting sources in conversation: grouping studies that agree, contrasting those that disagree, tracing how findings evolved, and identifying what no study has answered yet. Each paragraph should cite multiple sources making one shared point, not summarize one source at a time.
