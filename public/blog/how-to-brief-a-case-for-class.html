How to Brief a Case for Class: A Law Student Guide

The case brief format explained section by section, with a ready-to-use template

July 2026 • 9 min read

By the Sleek Academia tutoring team

menu_bookPart of our Assignment Help for Law Students →

Case briefing is the first real skill law school teaches, and the one students most often do badly for the longest. The purpose is widely misunderstood. A brief is not a summary of the opinion for its own sake. It is a training exercise in extraction: reading a thirty-page opinion and pulling out exactly the elements a lawyer needs to use that case as authority. When the cold call comes, the brief is what lets you answer in thirty seconds instead of three panicked minutes.

The format is standardized because the skill is standardized. This guide walks through each section of the brief, explains what belongs in it and what does not, and ends with a template you can reuse for every assigned case.

Key Takeaways

  • A brief is a compression exercise: one page, every section earning its place.
  • Include only legally significant facts, the ones the holding turns on.
  • Frame the issue as a precise question that combines the legal point with the key facts.
  • Distinguish the rule (general principle) from the holding (this case's answer).
  • Capture the dissent's core objection in one or two sentences; exams live there.

Before You Brief: How to Read the Case

Brief the case on your second read, not your first. On the first read, read like a lawyer watching a story unfold: who are the parties, what happened, who is suing whom for what, and what does the court decide. Skim nothing structural: the caption tells you the parties and the court, the first paragraphs usually state the issue, and the final paragraphs state the disposition.

On the second read, read with the brief template beside you and fill each section as you go. Mark the procedural history in the margin, underline the sentence where the court states the rule, and bracket the facts the court repeats when it applies the law, because those are the facts the holding depends on. This two-pass method is slower for the first few weeks and dramatically faster once it becomes habit.

The Sections of a Case Brief

Caption and Citation

Record the case name, the court, the year, and the full citation in the format your course requires. This takes seconds and saves you from the classic mistake of writing a perfect brief of a case you can no longer identify. If your course requires Bluebook format, build the citation correctly the first time; our Bluebook citation guide covers case citation form.

Facts

The facts section is where most briefs go wrong, usually by including everything. Include only the legally significant facts: the ones the court's reasoning turns on, the ones that, if changed, would change the outcome. A useful filter is to write the facts after you have identified the holding, because the holding tells you which facts mattered. Include the parties' identities in functional terms (the buyer, the injured pedestrian, the arresting officer) rather than drowning the brief in surnames. Aim for four to eight sentences.

Procedural History

How did this case arrive at this court, and what happened below? The plaintiff sued for breach of contract; the trial court granted summary judgment for the defendant; the plaintiff appealed. Procedural posture matters more than first-year students expect, because standards of review and what the appellate court can actually decide both flow from it. Keep it to two or three sentences, ending with what this court is being asked to do.

Issue

The issue is the precise legal question the court must answer, framed as a question that marries the legal point to the key facts. The weak version: "whether there was a contract." The strong version: "whether a series of emails that names the product, quantity, and price, but leaves delivery terms open, constitutes a binding contract under the UCC." The strong version tells you exactly what to look for in the holding, and it is the version that prepares you for the professor's follow-up questions. One issue per brief where possible; if the opinion decides several, number them.

Rule

State the legal principle the court applies: the elements of a test, the relevant statute, or the precedent that governs. Distinguish this from the holding. The rule is general and transfers to future cases; the holding is the court's answer for these parties. Where the court synthesizes a rule from several earlier cases, note that synthesis, because professors often test whether you noticed the court refining the doctrine.

Reasoning (Application or Analysis)

This is the heart of the brief and the section that deserves the most space. Trace how the court moved from rule to holding: which facts it treated as decisive, how it handled the precedent each side cited, and why it rejected the losing argument. Do not just state that the court applied the test; capture the pivot, the sentence or two where the opinion turns. If the court distinguished a seemingly contrary case, record the distinction, because that is exactly the move you will need to make on exams and in practice.

Holding and Conclusion

State the holding in one sentence as the answer to your issue question, plus the disposition: affirmed, reversed, remanded. "Yes: under the UCC, the emails formed a binding contract because they specified quantity, the only strictly required term. Reversed and remanded." When issue, holding, and disposition line up this cleanly, your brief is doing its job.

Concurrence and Dissent

When separate opinions exist, capture each in one or two sentences: what does the dissenter think the majority got wrong, and what rule would they apply instead? Dissents earn their place in your brief because they expose the contested ground in the case, and contested ground is where class discussion and exam hypotheticals concentrate. A dissent also frequently becomes majority law later, something professors enjoy pointing out.

A Ready-to-Use Case Brief Template

Copy this skeleton into your notes and fill it for each assigned case:

  1. Case name, court, year, citation
  2. Facts: legally significant facts only, four to eight sentences
  3. Procedural history: how the case got here, what happened below, what is asked of this court
  4. Issue: the precise legal question, keyed to the key facts
  5. Rule: the governing principle, test, or statute
  6. Reasoning: how the court applied the rule to these facts, including the pivot point and treatment of contrary authority
  7. Holding: the one-sentence answer, plus the disposition
  8. Concurrence / dissent: the core disagreement in one or two sentences each
  9. Your notes: why this case is in the casebook at this point, and how it connects to the cases around it

That final line is the difference between briefing and understanding. Casebooks sequence cases deliberately, and the brief that notes the sequence ("this narrows the rule from last week's case") is the one that pays off at exam time.

Common Briefing Mistakes

  • Copying the opinion's language. A brief in the court's words proves you can highlight. A brief in your words proves you understand.
  • Fact overload. If the facts section is longer than the reasoning section, the balance is wrong.
  • Vague issues. An issue without the key facts cannot be answered by a holding, and it will not help you in a cold call.
  • Skipping the procedural posture. Many holdings only make sense, and only have their precedential weight, because of the procedural stage.
  • Ignoring the dissent. You are briefing half the conversation.

Briefing is a skill with a short learning curve and a long payoff. The same extraction discipline feeds directly into essay exams, where you will run the analysis in reverse, and into the IRAC paragraphs covered in our guide on how to write a law essay. Frequently Asked Questions

How long should a case brief be?

A working case brief for class preparation is usually one page or less. If your brief runs several pages, you are copying the opinion rather than distilling it. The discipline of the brief is compression: legally significant facts, one precise issue, the rule, the core reasoning, and the holding.

What is the difference between a holding and a rule in a case brief?

The rule is the general legal principle the court applies, often drawn from precedent or statute. The holding is the court's answer to the specific issue in this case: who won, on what ground, at this procedural stage. The rule transfers to future cases; the holding resolves this one.

Should I include the dissent in my case brief?

Yes, whenever a dissent exists and your professor emphasizes it, and often even when they do not. A dissent reveals which facts and which framing the majority had to reject, which is frequently where class discussion and exam questions focus. One or two sentences capturing the dissent's core objection is enough.

Is briefing cases still worth the time after the first year?

Most students shift from full briefs to book briefing (annotating the margin with facts, issue, rule, and holding) once the skill becomes automatic. The value of briefing is the reading discipline it builds: identifying what legally matters in an opinion is the same skill exams and practice demand.
