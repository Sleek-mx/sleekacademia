How to Write a Technical Report That Gets Results

A practical framework for engineering, IT, and science students

Technical reports are the most common form of written assessment in engineering, IT, and applied science courses. Unlike essays, which develop an argument through prose, technical reports communicate findings, analysis, and recommendations in a format designed for quick navigation and clear comprehension. A well-structured technical report can earn top marks even when the underlying work is modest, while a poorly organised report can sink excellent work.

Step 1: Analyse Your Audience

Before you write a single word, identify who will read your report. The most common audiences are:

  • Lecturers and assessors -- they know the subject but are evaluating your methodology, critical thinking, and communication skills.
  • Industry supervisors -- they care about actionable recommendations and practical feasibility.
  • Mixed audiences -- some readers are technical, others are managerial. Your report must serve both.

The key implication for your writing: never assume your reader knows the specific context of your project. Define acronyms, explain technical terms that are not universal, and state your assumptions explicitly.

Step 2: Choose the Right Structure

While report structures vary by discipline, most technical reports follow this core sequence:

Section What It Should Contain
Executive Summary Problem, approach, key findings, recommendations — written last, placed first
Introduction Background, objectives, scope, report outline
Methodology How the investigation was performed, tools and techniques used
Results / Findings Data, observations, and technical analysis presented objectively
Discussion Interpretation of results, comparison with expectations or benchmarks
Recommendations Actionable steps derived from the discussion
Conclusion Summary of achievements, limitations, future work
References & Appendices Citations and supplementary material

Some assignments combine Results and Discussion into a single section. Check your brief and follow the required structure if one is specified.

Step 3: Use Diagrams and Visuals Strategically

A good diagram can replace paragraphs of text. In technical reports, visuals are not decorative -- they are analytical tools. Use them to show relationships (flowcharts), compare data (bar charts, line graphs), illustrate systems (architecture diagrams), and present hierarchies (tree diagrams).

Every visual must be numbered (Figure 1, Table 2), given a descriptive caption, and referenced in the text. Never insert a diagram without explaining its significance. The text should tell the reader what to look for in the visual.

Diagram Quality Checklist

  • Is the resolution high enough to read all labels?
  • Are all axes labelled with units?
  • Is the colour scheme accessible to colour-blind readers?
  • Does the caption explain what the diagram shows, not just what it is?
  • Is the diagram placed after the paragraph that first references it?

Step 4: Write Clear, Precise Language

Technical writing values clarity over elegance. Use short sentences, active voice where appropriate, and precise terminology. Avoid vague phrases like "a significant amount" when you can write "47 percent" or "2.3 litres per second."

Each paragraph should make one point. Start with a topic sentence that tells the reader what the paragraph covers, then support it with evidence or explanation. If a paragraph runs longer than six sentences, consider splitting it.

Common Language Pitfalls

  • Passive overuse: "The test was conducted" is fine occasionally, but "We conducted the test" is clearer and shorter.
  • Nominalisation: "Conducted an analysis of" should be "analysed."
  • Jargon without definition: Define specialised terms on first use.
  • Ambiguous pronouns: "This shows that..." -- what is "this"? Be specific.

Step 5: Review and Revise Systematically

Before submitting, run through this review checklist. It catches the most common reasons technical reports lose marks.

Check What to Look For
Structure Does the report follow the required format? Are all sections present?
Executive summary Does it capture the key elements without introducing new information?
Figures and tables Are all figures numbered, captioned, and referenced in the text?
References Are all citations complete and in the required style (IEEE, APA, Harvard)?
Spelling and grammar Have you proofread for typos, subject-verb agreement, and punctuation?
Consistency Are terminology, formatting, and heading styles consistent throughout?
Appendices Is all appendix content referenced in the main text? Remove unreferenced material.

Technical report writing is a skill that improves with each attempt. By analysing your audience, following a clear structure, using visuals intentionally, and reviewing systematically, you will produce reports that communicate your technical work effectively and earn the marks it deserves. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a technical report and an essay?

A technical report presents findings and recommendations based on investigation, using headings, figures, and tables. An essay argues a thesis in continuous prose. Reports are structured for skimming; essays are structured for linear reading.

How many diagrams should a technical report include?

Include a diagram wherever it communicates information more clearly than text. A 3,000-word technical report typically includes 3 to 6 diagrams or tables. Every figure must be labelled and referenced in the text.

Who reads a technical report?

Technical reports typically have two audiences: technical readers who assess the methodology and findings, and managerial readers who focus on the executive summary and recommendations. Write clearly enough for both.

Should I write the executive summary first or last?

Write the executive summary last. You cannot summarise a report accurately until the report is complete. Once written, place it at the front so that readers see it first.
