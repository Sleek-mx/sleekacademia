How to Write a Research Paper: The Complete Guide

From a blank prompt to a finished, cited draft — a process that holds up under deadline pressure

July 2026 • 11 min read

By the Sleek Academia tutoring team

A research paper looks enormous from the outside and mechanical from the inside. Almost every strong paper you have ever read was produced the same way: the writer broke the work into a short sequence of decisions — topic, thesis, sources, outline, draft, revision — and made each decision before moving to the next. Students who struggle rarely lack intelligence. They are usually trying to make all six decisions at once, at 1 a.m., with the deadline already inside twenty-four hours.

This guide walks through the process in the order that actually works. Follow it once, deliberately, and you will have a repeatable system for every research paper that comes after — in nursing, law, business, or any other field that asks you to argue from evidence.

1. Start With the Assignment, Not the Topic

Before you brainstorm a single idea, read the prompt like a contract. Instructors hide concrete requirements inside polite paragraphs, and missing any one of them costs easy points. Pull out and write down:

  • Length. Page or word count, and whether the reference list and appendices count toward it.
  • Source requirements. How many sources, what type (peer-reviewed, primary, current within five years), and what is banned (textbooks, websites, Wikipedia).
  • Citation style. APA 7, MLA 9, Chicago, or Harvard. This shapes your formatting from the first note you take — our APA 7 formatting guide covers the most common one in detail.
  • Paper type. Analytical (break a topic down and evaluate it), argumentative (defend a position), or expository (explain). Each demands a different thesis.
  • Milestones. Topic approval, annotated bibliography, outline, or draft deadlines that arrive before the final one.

Five minutes of this saves the classic disaster: writing a beautiful paper that answers a question nobody asked.

2. Choose a Topic You Can Actually Argue

The most common topic mistake is scope. "Social media and mental health" is not a topic — it is a library. A workable research paper topic passes three tests: it is narrow enough to cover in your page limit, rich enough that credible sources exist, and contestable enough that a reasonable person could disagree.

Narrow by applying constraints. Take a broad interest and limit it by population, place, time period, or mechanism. "Social media and mental health" becomes "the relationship between short-form video use and sleep quality in first-year college students." Now you have something you can actually prove or complicate within eight pages.

Before committing, spend twenty minutes on a quick literature scan in your library database. If you find either nothing or forty thousand results, adjust the scope. You want a conversation you can join, not a void or a stadium.

3. Develop a Working Thesis

Your thesis is the single sentence your entire paper exists to defend. Everything else — every source, every paragraph — either serves it or gets cut. A reliable formula is: claim + because + significance. State your position, gesture at the reasoning, and imply why it matters.

Compare the weak and strong versions:

  • Weak: "This paper will discuss telehealth in nursing." That is an announcement, not an argument.
  • Strong: "Telehealth follow-ups reduce 30-day readmissions for heart failure patients because they catch fluid retention earlier, which makes them a staffing priority rather than a convenience."

The strong version can be argued against, names its evidence, and tells you what the body paragraphs must prove. Call it a working thesis deliberately: expect to sharpen it after your reading, and again after your first draft. A thesis that never changes usually means you stopped thinking.

4. Find Sources That Survive Scrutiny

Research papers are graded on the quality of their evidence as much as the quality of their prose. Build your source base in layers:

  • Start in the library databases — PubMed or CINAHL for nursing, Westlaw or HeinOnline for law, JSTOR, EBSCO, and Google Scholar for most other fields. Filter for peer-reviewed and by date.
  • Mine the references. One good recent article is a map to twenty more. Read its reference list for foundational work and its "cited by" list for the newest developments.
  • Evaluate before you commit. For each source, ask: who wrote it, where was it published, how recent is it, and does it present evidence or opinion? A source you cannot defend in one sentence does not belong in your paper.

As you read, take notes with the citation attached first. Record the author, year, page number, and whether the note is a quote, a paraphrase, or your own reaction. Students who skip this step spend their final hour before the deadline hunting page numbers — or worse, guessing at them.

5. Build an Outline Before You Draft

An outline is not bureaucracy. It is the moment you find out whether your argument actually works, while fixing it is still free. A standard research paper outline looks like this:

  1. Introduction — context, the gap or problem, your thesis, and a one-sentence preview of the structure.
  2. Background or literature review — what is already known, organized thematically. Longer papers sometimes expand this into a full section of its own; our guide on how to write a literature review covers that form in depth.
  3. Body sections — one per major claim supporting the thesis, each with its own mini-claim, evidence, and analysis.
  4. Counterargument — the strongest objection to your thesis, stated fairly and answered with evidence.
  5. Conclusion — the argument restated in light of the evidence, plus the implication: what should the reader now think or do differently?

Write the outline as full sentences, one per paragraph you plan to draft. If a section of the outline feels thin, that is where your research is thin — go back to the databases now, not after 1,500 words of drafting.

6. Write the First Draft Fast

Separate drafting from editing. The first draft has exactly one job: to exist. Write it in the order your brain prefers — many strong writers draft the body first, then the conclusion, then the introduction last, when they finally know what they are introducing.

Build body paragraphs with a repeatable structure: point, evidence, analysis. Open with the paragraph's claim, present the source evidence (quoted or paraphrased and cited), then spend at least as many sentences analyzing the evidence as presenting it. The most common weakness in student papers is quote stacking — three citations back to back with no reasoning between them. Your grade lives in the analysis, not the quotations.

Mark gaps instead of stopping for them. A bracketed note like [find the 2023 readmission statistic] keeps the momentum moving. Perfectionism during a first draft is how a six-page paper becomes a three-week ordeal.

7. Write the Introduction and Conclusion Last

Introductions written first are almost always rewritten, because you cannot introduce an argument you have not finished making. Once the body is drafted, open the paper with three moves: establish the topic's significance in two or three sentences, name the problem or debate your sources circle around, and close the paragraph with your thesis and a one-sentence map of what follows. No throat-clearing ("Since the dawn of time..."), no dictionary definitions, no announcements ("This paper will discuss...").

The conclusion runs the introduction in reverse: thesis restated in light of the evidence (never copy-pasted), the main findings compressed into two or three sentences, and the answer to the reader's final question — so what? End with an implication, a recommendation, or the question your paper leaves open. Introduce no new sources here. If a new piece of evidence appears in your conclusion, that is a sign it belongs in the body.

8. Integrate Sources With Signal Phrases

How a source enters your sentence changes how credible it sounds. Dropped quotations — a quote inserted between two of your sentences with no introduction — read as if the source is interrupting you. Use signal phrases that tell the reader who is speaking and how much weight to give them: Aiken and colleagues found..., In a 2024 systematic review, Dall'Ora et al. concluded..., Critics such as Nguyen dispute this, arguing... Vary the verbs. Found reports a result; argued marks a position; demonstrated claims strong evidence; suggested signals caution. Your choice of verb is analysis happening at the sentence level.

Keep the ratio in your favor: your own sentences should outnumber quoted and paraphrased material comfortably. A useful ceiling is one quotation per paragraph, and no paragraph that opens and closes with someone else's words.

9. What to Do When You Get Stuck

Three stall points account for most abandoned drafts. If you cannot find a thesis, your topic is too broad — go back to step two and narrow it. If your outline will not hold, your research is thin in one section — the empty column in your notes tells you which one. If the draft stalls mid-paragraph, you are probably editing instead of writing: switch to writing badly on purpose for ten minutes, because momentum matters more than polish at this stage. And if the stall is really the calendar — the paper is due in twelve hours and you are still on step one — that is a logistics problem, not a writing problem, and it is what our assignment help service is built for.

10. Cite as You Go

Citations are cheapest at the moment of use. Every time you drop in a quotation or paraphrase, attach the in-text citation immediately and add the full entry to your reference list in the same sitting. This habit eliminates two of the most common ways students lose points: accidental plagiarism from a lost source, and a reference list assembled from memory at the end.

Know which style your course requires before you draft, because the styles differ in more than punctuation. APA foregrounds the year, MLA the page number, and Chicago offers both notes and author-date systems. If APA 7 is your required style, keep our APA formatting guide with examples open in a second tab while you work.

11. Revise in Three Passes

Trying to fix everything in one read-through is why revision feels hopeless. Professional writers revise in passes, from the largest structure to the smallest sentence:

  • Pass one — argument. Read only the thesis and the first sentence of each paragraph. Does the sequence prove the thesis? Is anything missing, repeated, or off-topic? Move and cut whole paragraphs here.
  • Pass two — evidence. Check every claim against a source. Confirm that quotations are exact, paraphrases are genuinely reworded, and every in-text citation has a matching reference entry.
  • Pass three — surface. Grammar, spelling, punctuation, formatting, page numbers, headings. Reading the paper aloud, or having your device read it to you, catches what silent reading skips.

The Mistakes That Cost the Most Points

  • A topic instead of a thesis. If your introduction ends by announcing what the paper "will discuss," you have not committed to an argument yet.
  • Quote stacking. Evidence without analysis reads as assembly, not thinking. Every quotation needs at least one sentence of your reasoning after it.
  • Sources that only agree. A paper with no counterargument looks unresearched. Address the strongest objection directly and your position gets more credible, not less.
  • The midnight bibliography. Building the reference list from memory guarantees errors. Compile it as you research, not after you draft.
  • Editing while drafting. Polishing sentence three for forty minutes while six pages remain unwritten is the most expensive habit in student writing.

The process above is the whole secret: brief, topic, thesis, sources, outline, draft, revise. None of the steps is difficult. All of them are fatal when skipped. Frequently Asked Questions

How long should a research paper be?

Follow the assignment brief first. Undergraduate research papers typically run 6 to 12 pages (roughly 1,650 to 3,300 words at 275 words per page), while graduate work can reach 20 pages or more. If no length is given, ask the instructor before you start.

How many sources do I need for a research paper?

A common baseline is one to two quality sources per page, but the brief rules. Prioritize peer-reviewed journal articles and academic books, and make sure every source you cite actually appears in the paper.

What is the difference between a research paper and an essay?

An essay argues from your own reasoning and a small set of assigned readings. A research paper builds its argument from a systematic review of outside sources, with every claim traced to published evidence.

Can I use first person in a research paper?

In most disciplines, yes, sparingly and with purpose. APA 7 explicitly allows first person when describing your own actions or position. Avoid using it for opinions that should be grounded in evidence instead.

How do I know if my thesis statement is strong enough?

Test it three ways: it can be argued against, it is narrow enough to prove within your page limit, and every body paragraph connects back to it. If a reasonable reader could shrug and say so what, keep sharpening.
