How to Write a Nursing Care Plan (With Template)

Assessment to evaluation, the ADPIE way — plus a complete worked example you can adapt

July 2026 • 10 min read

By the Sleek Academia tutoring team

menu_bookPart of your nursing prep? See the Complete NCLEX-RN Prep Guide →

Every nursing student remembers the first care plan that took six hours. Not because the patient was complex, but because nobody had explained that a care plan is just the nursing process written down in a fixed order: Assess, Diagnose, Plan, Implement, Evaluate — ADPIE. Once you see the document as five linked decisions instead of one giant form, it stops being intimidating. This guide walks through each step with the formats instructors actually grade, then shows a complete worked example you can adapt for your own patients.

Step 1: Assessment — Gather the Right Data

Everything in your care plan traces back to assessment data, so collect it in two buckets:

  • Subjective data — what the patient tells you: "I feel like I cannot catch my breath," pain ratings, nausea, anxiety. Quote the patient where you can.
  • Objective data — what you observe and measure: vital signs, SpO2, lung sounds, lab values, wound appearance, intake and output.

Use a systematic framework so nothing is missed: head-to-toe, body systems, or Gordon's Functional Health Patterns, whichever your program teaches. Pull from every source available to you — the patient interview, your own physical assessment, the chart's history and lab work, and the family's report where relevant — and label each piece of data with where it came from. The discipline that matters most is separating data from interpretation. "SpO2 89% on room air" is data. "The patient is not oxygenating well" is interpretation — that belongs in the next step. Mixing them early is how students end up with diagnoses their assessment column cannot support.

Step 2: Nursing Diagnosis — Use the PES Format

A nursing diagnosis is not a medical diagnosis. The physician diagnoses pneumonia; the nurse diagnoses how the patient is responding to it — impaired gas exchange, activity intolerance, anxiety. That distinction is the single most tested concept in care plan grading.

Academic care plans use the NANDA-I taxonomy in PES format:

  • P — Problem: the NANDA-I label, such as Impaired gas exchange.
  • E — Etiology: the cause or contributing factors, joined by "related to" — related to ventilation-perfusion imbalance secondary to pneumonia.
  • S — Signs and symptoms: your defining assessment data, joined by "as evidenced by" (AEB) — AEB SpO2 89% on room air, respiratory rate 28, use of accessory muscles, and patient report of breathlessness.

The full statement reads: Impaired gas exchange related to ventilation-perfusion imbalance as evidenced by SpO2 89%, tachypnea, accessory muscle use, and patient report of breathlessness. For risk diagnoses — risk for falls, risk for infection — drop the S. The problem has not happened yet, so there is no evidence: Risk for infection related to compromised skin integrity and invasive lines. Prioritize your diagnoses using ABCs and Maslow: airway, breathing, and circulation problems come before comfort and psychosocial ones, exactly as the NCLEX will demand — see our guide on passing the NCLEX on your first attempt for how that same logic is tested.

Prioritizing Multiple Diagnoses

A real patient generates a dozen possible nursing diagnoses; a care plan assignment usually asks for two to four. Ordering them is itself graded clinical reasoning. Work down this hierarchy:

  • Physiological ABCs first. Anything touching airway, breathing, or circulation outranks everything else. Impaired gas exchange beats chronic pain.
  • Safety next. Risk for falls, risk for injury, and risk for infection follow immediately after physiological threats.
  • Then comfort, mobility, nutrition, and elimination. Acute pain, impaired physical mobility, imbalanced nutrition.
  • Psychosocial and educational last — but not optional. Anxiety, deficient knowledge, and ineffective health management are legitimate diagnoses; they simply do not jump the queue ahead of a physiological one.

Notice this is exactly the prioritization logic the NCLEX tests with its "which patient does the nurse see first" questions — the care plan and the exam are training the same judgment, which is why instructors weight it so heavily. If you are building that judgment for the exam itself, our first-attempt NCLEX guide goes deeper on prioritization frameworks.

Step 3: Planning — Write SMART Outcomes

Goals and outcomes are where vague writing gets caught. An outcome must be SMART — specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound — and written from the patient's perspective, not the nurse's:

  • Short-term: "The patient will maintain SpO2 at 92% or greater on prescribed oxygen within 24 hours."
  • Long-term: "The patient will demonstrate effective coughing and clear breath sounds by discharge."

Two traps to avoid. First, never write the outcome as a nursing action — "monitor oxygen saturation" is an intervention, not an outcome. Second, every outcome must be measurable by data you can actually collect: SpO2 numbers, pain scores, distances walked, temperatures. "The patient will feel better" cannot be evaluated; "the patient will report pain at 3/10 or less within one hour of analgesia" can. If your program uses NOC (Nursing Outcomes Classification), the same logic applies — pick the label, then define the indicators with numbers. And check achievability against your actual patient: an outcome the patient cannot physically reach in the timeframe is not ambitious, it is ungradeable.

Step 4: Interventions — With Rationales That Actually Explain

For each diagnosis, academic care plans typically expect three to five nursing interventions, each followed by a rationale with a citation. Interventions must be independent or collaborative nursing actions, specific enough that another nurse could perform them exactly: "Administer prescribed bronchodilator" is too thin; "Administer albuterol 2.5 mg via nebulizer as prescribed, monitoring heart rate before and after" is an intervention.

The rationale is where most marks are lost. A rationale explains why the intervention works, physiologically — not what you will do, restated:

  • Weak rationale: "Elevating the head of the bed helps the patient breathe."
  • Strong rationale: "Fowler's position drops the diaphragm and increases lung expansion, improving ventilation-perfusion matching (Ackley et al., 2022)."

Cite your fundamentals or medical-surgical textbook, a NANDA-I/NIC/NOC text, or current evidence in whatever style your program requires — our APA 7 guide covers the citation mechanics if that is your program's format. Generic rationale-free lists copied from the internet are the fastest way to lose the assignment's credibility.

Step 5: Evaluation — Close the Loop

Evaluation restates each outcome and records whether it was met, partially met, or not met, with the data that proves it: "Goal met. SpO2 maintained at 93–95% on 2L nasal cannula over 24 hours; respiratory rate 20." For unmet goals, say what you would revise — a new intervention, a new timeframe, or a reassessed diagnosis. On paper-based student assignments where the patient is a case study, you still write the evaluation column: project it from the evidence and say so. The evaluation step is what turns your document from a form into clinical reasoning, and it is the same recognize-cues-then-evaluate-outcomes loop the Next Generation NCLEX scores you on.

A full assignment usually repeats this table for two to four diagnoses, ordered by priority. Keep every column internally consistent: the evidence in your diagnosis must come from your assessment, the outcomes must fix the diagnosis, the interventions must reach the outcomes, and the evaluation must measure the outcomes. That chain is what instructors are really grading.

A Second Example: The Risk Diagnosis

Risk diagnoses confuse students because the S disappears. Here is the same chain run for a risk statement on the same patient, now post-fall-risk-assessment:

  • Assessment: Objective — age 78, new opioid analgesia started yesterday, unsteady gait observed, Morse Fall Scale score 65, hypotension 102/64. Subjective — "I get dizzy when I stand up."
  • Diagnosis: Risk for falls related to orthostatic hypotension, new opioid therapy, and impaired mobility. No AEB — the fall has not happened.
  • Outcome: The patient will remain free from falls throughout the hospital stay.
  • Interventions: Institute fall precautions (bed low, call light in reach, non-skid socks); assist with all transfers and ambulation; monitor blood pressure lying and standing; review sedation timing with the provider; keep the environment free of clutter. Each with its rationale cited.
  • Evaluation: Projected — patient remains fall-free; precautions continued.

Concept Maps and Other Formats Your School May Use

The five-column table is the most common academic format, but it is not the only one. Concept maps ask you to place the primary medical diagnosis in the center and branch nursing diagnoses, data, and interventions outward with linking arrows — the point is showing how problems connect, not listing them. Narrative care plans present the same ADPIE logic in prose. Some programs use standardized three-part formats built around NIC and NOC labels rather than free-text outcomes and interventions. Whatever the wrapper, the underlying chain never changes: data to diagnosis, diagnosis to outcomes, outcomes to interventions, interventions to evaluation. If your school's template differs from the example above, map each column of this guide onto their headings and the content transfers directly.

From Classroom to Clinical: Keeping the Plan Alive

On the floor, care plans live in the EHR rather than in five neat columns, and many are generated from standardized templates — but the reasoning is identical, which is why programs keep assigning the long-form version. Two habits transfer directly. First, individualize every standardized plan: a template intervention that does not match your patient's data is a documentation liability, and preceptors notice copy-forward care plans immediately. Second, update the plan as the patient changes. A care plan is a shift-by-shift document, not an admission-day artifact — resolved problems get discontinued, new data generates new diagnoses, and the evaluation column from yesterday becomes the assessment column of today. Students who treat the academic care plan as busywork miss that it is rehearsing the exact judgment cycle they will run every shift.

The Mistakes That Cost the Most Points

  • Writing the medical diagnosis as the nursing diagnosis. "Pneumonia" is not a nursing diagnosis. "Impaired gas exchange" is.
  • "Related to" statements naming a medical diagnosis as the cause. Connect the etiology to the pathophysiology (ventilation-perfusion imbalance), not just the disease label.
  • Outcomes written as nursing tasks. Outcomes belong to the patient; interventions belong to the nurse.
  • Rationale-free interventions. Every intervention needs its why, cited. No exceptions in academic work.
  • AEB statements with no data. If your assessment column does not contain the evidence, the diagnosis is unsupported.
  • Copy-paste care plans. Instructors recognize recycled content instantly, and it misses your patient's actual data. Build from assessment every time.

Master the chain — assessment to diagnosis to outcomes to interventions to evaluation — and care plans become the most predictable assignment in nursing school. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a nursing diagnosis and a medical diagnosis?

A medical diagnosis names the disease, such as pneumonia. A nursing diagnosis describes the patient's response to the condition that nurses can treat independently, such as impaired gas exchange or activity intolerance. Care plans are built on nursing diagnoses.

How many interventions should a care plan include?

Three to five interventions per nursing diagnosis is the usual expectation for student care plans, each with an evidence-based rationale. Quality and specificity matter more than quantity.

Do nursing interventions need rationales and citations?

In academic care plans, yes. Each intervention should be followed by a rationale explaining why it works, cited to a textbook or evidence-based source. Clinical care plans on the floor typically omit rationales.

What is the PES format for a nursing diagnosis?

PES stands for Problem, Etiology, and Signs and symptoms: the NANDA-I label, related to the cause or contributing factors, as evidenced by the defining assessment data. Risk diagnoses use only the problem and etiology, since no symptoms have occurred yet.

How do I write a SMART goal for a care plan?

Make it Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound, and write it from the patient's perspective: The patient will maintain SpO2 at 92 percent or higher within 24 hours, not the nurse will monitor oxygen saturation.
